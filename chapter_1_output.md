# Chapter I — Introduction

# 1.1 Historical Background: The Partition of India

The Partition of India in 1947 represents one of the most cataclysmic events of the twentieth century, a moment of national liberation inextricably intertwined with unprecedented human tragedy. While the independence of India from British colonial rule marked the culmination of decades of nationalist struggle, the simultaneous division of the subcontinent into the sovereign states of India and Pakistan unleashed waves of communal violence, mass migration, and displacement that fundamentally transformed South Asian society. Understanding this historical context is essential for analyzing how Khushwant Singh's *Train to Pakistan* engages with the lived realities of Partition, for the novel emerges precisely from the intersection of political upheaval and human suffering that defined this period.

The political origins of Partition can be traced to the complex dynamics of British colonial governance and the rise of competing nationalist movements in the early twentieth century. The British strategy of "divide and rule" systematically exploited religious and communal differences as a means of maintaining imperial control, a policy that planted the seeds of future conflict between Hindu and Muslim communities [4, p. 2]. As the independence movement gained momentum, the Indian National Congress, representing a broadly secular nationalist vision, found itself increasingly at odds with the Muslim League, which demanded separate political representation for Muslims. The high politics of these negotiations—the interactions between British authorities, Congress leaders such as Jawaharlal Nehru, and Muslim League figures such as Muhammad Ali Jinnah—ultimately determined the contours of Partition, yet these elite-level decisions bore little relation to the everyday realities of ordinary people across the subcontinent [7, p. 2]. The historian David Gilmartin has emphasized that the "high politics of partition… was uninfluenced by the everyday politics of local life," a disjuncture that would prove catastrophic when borders were drawn with little regard for the complex demographic realities of the Punjab and Bengal regions [7, p. 2].

When Partition finally occurred in August 1947, the violence that erupted was staggering in its scale and brutality. Estimates indicate that approximately one million people lost their lives in the communal massacres that accompanied the division, while ten million others were forcibly displaced from their ancestral homes and became refugees [3, p. 1]. Additionally, an estimated 75,000 women were abducted and raped during this period, though the true figure may be considerably higher [3, p. 1]. This violence was not a spontaneous eruption of primordial hatred but was systematically produced through a combination of political manipulation, propaganda, and the calculated actions of communal interest groups. As Gyanendra Pandey has observed, nationalist historiography tends to attribute such violence to "criminal elements" and "propagandists" who filled "the minds of the people with poisonous insanity," yet this explanation obscures the deeper structural causes of communal conflict [6, p. 2]. Economic rivalries played a significant role, with competition over resources and property often assuming communal dimensions; in both *Tamas* and *Train to Pakistan*, economic motives are shown to underlie much of the violence, as "religion is no consideration among buyers and sellers" and "profit is the overwhelming motif" driving communal tension [6, p. 2].

The experience of migration and displacement constitutes another crucial dimension of Partition's historical legacy. The largest mass migration in human history occurred as Hindus and Sikhs fled from areas that became Pakistan, while Muslims moved in the opposite direction toward the newly created state. This movement was not a peaceful relocation but a desperate flight from violence, with refugees traveling on foot, by bullock cart, and on overcrowded trains that were frequently attacked by armed mobs. The train, which had previously functioned as a symbol of connection and progress, became a vessel of death: "ghost trains" arrived at their destinations carrying only corpses, their passengers having been massacred en route [1, p. 1]. In *Train to Pakistan*, this transformation is rendered with stark power when the ghost train from Pakistan arrives in the fictional village of Mano Majra, carrying the bodies of slaughtered Hindus and Sikhs. Singh's description captures the moment when abstract rumors of violence become undeniable reality: "Everyone in Mano Majra knew by then that the train was carrying the dead" [1, p. 1]. This image crystallizes the horror of Partition, demonstrating how the machinery of modern life was repurposed for destruction.

The novel's portrayal of this violence is significant precisely because it refuses to reduce Partition to an abstract historical event. Rather than focusing on political leaders or high-level negotiations, Singh foregrounds the lived experience of ordinary people whose lives are shattered by communal violence, portraying Partition as "a human tragedy marked by fear, displacement, and moral collapse" [1, p. 1]. Through the microcosm of Mano Majra, a village where Sikhs, Muslims, and Hindus had "lived there in peace for generations," Singh exposes how centuries-old communal harmony disintegrates under the pressure of political manipulation and mass hysteria [1, p. 1]. Before Partition intrudes upon it, Mano Majra exists as a symbol of rural coexistence, where religion functions not as a source of conflict but as "a cultural marker integrated into daily life," and where the rhythm of the village is governed not by religious differences but by the timetable of trains passing nearby [1, p. 1]. This initial harmony makes the subsequent descent into violence all the more devastating, as it demonstrates how rapidly and completely communal trust can be destroyed.

One of the novel's most significant contributions to understanding Partition violence is its insistence that communal conflict is not spontaneous but is systematically produced through fear and retaliation. After the ghost train arrives, trust between communities erodes with alarming speed; Muslims who had lived peacefully in Mano Majra for generations are suddenly viewed as potential enemies. Singh captures this psychological shift succinctly: "The fact was that the Muslims were afraid. The Sikhs were afraid too" [1, p. 1]. Fear replaces empathy and reason as the primary emotion driving human action, and violence becomes "less an expression of hatred than a desperate response to insecurity and uncertainty" [1, p. 1]. This psychological realism complicates any simplistic moral judgment of the characters, suggesting that ordinary people are capable of terrible acts not because they are inherently evil but because they are terrified. Hannah Arendt's observation that "no wicked heart, a relatively rare phenomenon, is necessary to cause great evil" finds powerful illustration in Singh's novel, which demonstrates how structural conditions and collective emotions can produce atrocity without requiring demonic individuals [3, p. 1].

The representational challenges posed by Partition have profound implications for historiography and literature alike. Official nationalist history has tended to foreground the grand narrative of national liberation while marginalizing the traumatic details of Partition violence [3, p. 1]. School textbooks on both sides of the border have often minimized or elided the horrors of 1947, focusing instead on the achievements of independence and the heroism of elite leaders [8, p. 1]. Against this background of institutional forgetting, works of fiction have emerged as crucial sites of memory and contestation. As historians Ritu Menon and Kamla Bhasin have argued, "partition fiction (and some non-fiction) is almost the only social history we have of this time," and "it is in fiction, rather than any other genre, that we find an attempt to assimilate the full import of what Partition meant" [3, p. 1]. Literature thus performs a vital historiographical function, recovering experiences that official narratives suppress and giving voice to those whom elite-centered history ignores.

### 1.2 About Khushwant Singh

Khushwant Singh occupies a central position in the corpus of Indian English Partition literature, his work providing some of the earliest and most influential fictional accounts of the violence that accompanied the division of the subcontinent in 1947. An understanding of Singh’s biography, literary career, and thematic preoccupations is essential for analysing how *Train to Pakistan* engages with the complex interplay of communal violence and residual humanism. Singh’s personal experience of displacement, his professional training as a lawyer and journalist, and the particular historical moment in which he wrote all converge to shape the novel’s narrative strategies and ideological commitments.

Born in 1915 in Hadali, now in Pakistan, Khushwant Singh belonged to a Sikh family with a strong legal and administrative tradition. He studied at Government College, Lahore, and later at King’s College, London, before being called to the bar at the Inner Temple. Upon returning to India, Singh practised law at the Lahore High Court until the Partition forcibly displaced him and his family, placing him among “hundreds and thousands of other Sikhs and Hindus at the time, in Delhi” [4, p. 33]. This biographical detail is critical because it situates Singh not as a detached observer but as a victim-participant in the very events he would later fictionalise. Rituparna Roy emphasises that Singh’s “being a lawyer, journalist, a Sikh, a victim” constituted the “affective circumstances” that inevitably influenced his literary production [2, p. 2]. His legal training equipped him with a precise, documentary eye for evidence and testimony, while his journalistic career fostered a preference for stark realism and immediacy. Indeed, Ralph J. Crane characterises Singh’s novel as resembling “sensational journalism,” a style that prioritises impact over nuance and that sometimes risks “emasculating Hindus and Muslims” through reductive portrayals [2, p. 2].

Singh’s contribution to Partition literature is foundational and widely acknowledged. When *Train to Pakistan* was published in 1956, it became “the first novel ever written on the theme of partition and partition violence” [4, p. 33]. Literary historians regard it as a “classic” and, in Salman Rushdie’s estimation, “the only good book on the theme” [9, p. 1]. The novel thus inaugurated a tradition of English-language fiction that sought to represent the unspeakable horrors of 1947, filling a lacuna left by official historiography, which long remained silent about the violence in favour of nationalist narratives of liberation. Singh’s work, alongside later texts by Bhisham Sahni, Amitav Ghosh, and others, forms part of what Urvashi Butalia and Gyanendra Pandey have described as the “other side” of Partition—the lived, traumatic memory that mainstream accounts tended to repress [1, p. 9]. However, as Beerendra Pandey argues, almost all Indian English Partition novels written before 1984 follow “a configuration along the line of nationalist history,” and Singh’s novel is no exception [4, p. 1]. This tension between offering a counter-narrative and reproducing nationalist historiographical frames is central to understanding the ideological complexity of Singh’s work.

Thematically, *Train to Pakistan* foregrounds the disintegration of communal harmony under the pressure of political manipulation and collective fear. Singh portrays the village of Mano Majra as a microcosm of pre-Partition rural coexistence, where “Sikhs and Muslims live side by side, bound by shared routines rather than religious divisions” [7, p. 1]. The initial tranquillity, governed by the rhythm of passing trains rather than sectarian difference, is shattered by the arrival of a “ghost train” carrying the corpses of massacred Hindus and Sikhs. This moment serves as a turning point: the train, once a symbol of connection, becomes a “harbinger of death” that transforms rumours into undeniable reality, instilling fear and suspicion [7, p. 1]. Singh’s representation of violence emphasises its systematic, non-spontaneous character. Fear replaces empathy, and communities that had coexisted for generations become polarised. The narrator notes that “the Muslims were afraid. The Sikhs were afraid too,” capturing a reciprocal paralysis in which violence is less an expression of primordial hatred than a desperate response to uncertainty [7, p. 1]. This psychological insight aligns with Hannah Arendt’s observation that “no wicked heart” is necessary to cause great evil; ordinary people, driven by fear and conformism, become complicit in atrocity [9, p. 1].

Despite the novel’s grim depiction of communal violence, Singh also gestures toward the possibility of humanism and sacrifice. The character of Juggat Singh, an erstwhile criminal who redeems himself by saving a trainload of Sikhs and Hindus from a Muslim mob, embodies a moral counterpoint to the prevailing brutality. Beerendra Pandey identifies a pattern in Sikh-authored Partition fiction: a “conscious foregrounding of Sikhs as civilized citizen vis-à-vis the barbaric Muslims through the configuration of the protagonist, Juggat Singh” [2, p. 2]. This formulation reveals the affective politics underpinning the narrative. The novel demonises the Muslim “other” even as it celebrates Sikh heroism, a strategy that, according to Dipak Raj Joshi, “follows the nationalist historiographies” of the writer, producing “a prose of demonization and denunciation of whom they consider the Other” [3, p. 1]. The sacrifice of Juggat Singh, while humanistic in its valorisation of selflessness, simultaneously reinforces communal stereotypes and exonerates the Sikh community from responsibility for violence.

Singh’s writing style combines stark realism with bitter irony, a technique that allows him to critique both political elites and communal fanaticism. The novel employs a third-person omniscient narrator who reports events with journalistic precision, often undercutting official narratives. In a significant passage, the magistrate Hukum Chand reflects sardonically on Prime Minister Nehru’s famous “tryst with destiny” speech, contrasting the ceremony of independence with the suffering of ordinary people: “Yes, Mr Prime Minister, you made your tryst, so did many others” [9, p. 1]. This ironic discrepancy exposes the gap between high politics and lived reality, a critique that echoes the historian David Gilmartin’s observation that Partition historiography long focused on “high politics” while ignoring “the everyday politics of local life” [2, p. 2]. Singh’s prose thus operates on two levels: it offers a visceral account of violence while simultaneously subverting the dominant nationalist narrative that celebrates independence without acknowledging its cost.

Critical reception of Singh’s work has been divided between those who laud his humanism and those who detect communal bias. Grishma M. Khobragade reads *Train to Pakistan* as a novel of “multicultural harmony,” arguing that Singh portrays “the integration of difference” and advocates for secular coexistence [2, p. 2]. Conversely, Joshi contends that Singh’s affective politics are “ethically tilted to the notions of community and nationhood,” so that the love depicted for one community concomitantly invites “antonymic affect for the other” [3, p. 1]. Marc Chatterji offers a more nuanced assessment, suggesting that the novel both “undermines and upholds mainstream representations of the Partition” [9, p. 1]. On one hand, it breaks the silence by depicting violence; on the other, it exonerates the villagers by attributing responsibility to “outsiders, criminals and secondary characters,” thereby “keeping violence at bay” and practising a form of “self-exoneration” [10, p. 5]. This dual movement—simultaneously revealing and rationalising—constitutes the novel’s ideological ambivalence.

In conclusion, Khushwant Singh’s life, literary background, and thematic concerns converge to produce a Partition narrative that is at once powerful and problematic. His personal experience of displacement, his journalistic eye for detail, and his commitment to documenting ordinary suffering give *Train to Pakistan* an authenticity that has secured its canonical status. Yet the novel also reproduces communal biases and nationalist historiographical frames, raising questions about the ethical responsibilities of trauma representation. Singh’s writing style—realistic, ironic, and emotionally charged—enables a critical engagement with official history while simultaneously limiting that critique through the affective politics of community allegiance. These tensions form the necessary backdrop for a detailed analysis of how *Train to Pakistan* represents violence and humanism, a task to which the following section turns.

# 1.3 Introduction to the Novel *Train to Pakistan*

Khushwant Singh's *Train to Pakistan* (1956) stands as one of the most significant fictional representations of the Partition of India, offering a microcosmic examination of how communal violence erupts within a previously harmonious rural community. Rather than focusing on political negotiations or elite decision-making, the novel foregrounds the lived experiences of ordinary villagers whose lives are irrevocably shattered by forces beyond their control [4, p. 1]. Through the carefully constructed setting of Mano Majra, Singh explores the central themes of violence, communalism, sacrifice, and humanism, demonstrating how centuries of coexistence can disintegrate under the pressure of political manipulation and collective fear. The novel's enduring power lies in its refusal to assign blame exclusively to one community, instead presenting Partition as a catastrophic breakdown of human values that implicates all parties involved [1, p. 1].

The setting of Mano Majra functions as a symbolic representation of pre-Partition rural India, where religious diversity coexisted within a framework of shared daily life. Singh describes the village as having "only one street" where "Muslims, Sikhs and a few Hindu families lived there in peace for generations" [4, p. 1]. This physical proximity and historical continuity establish a baseline of communal harmony against which the subsequent violence is measured. The rhythm of village life is governed not by religious differences but by the timetable of trains passing nearby, reinforcing a sense of order and predictability that Partition will violently disrupt [4, p. 1]. Significantly, all inhabitants of Mano Majra venerate the local deity, the *deo*, while both the Sikh guru Meet Singh and the Muslim Mullah Imam Baksh command respect across religious boundaries [7, p. 2]. This shared spiritual landscape suggests that religious identity, prior to Partition, functioned as a cultural marker rather than a source of conflict. The novel thus establishes that communal harmony is not an idealized fiction but a lived reality that can be destroyed by external forces.

The major characters of *Train to Pakistan* embody the moral complexities and conflicting loyalties that emerge during times of crisis. Juggut Singh, or Jugga, serves as the novel's central protagonist, a young Sikh with a criminal record who is known to the police as "Jugga Badmas" [7, p. 2]. Despite his lawless reputation, Jugga possesses a capacity for love and sacrifice that ultimately transcends communal boundaries. His romantic relationship with Nooran, the daughter of the Muslim mullah Imam Baksh, represents the possibility of human connection that defies religious division. Nooran herself embodies the vulnerability of women during Partition, her silent terror and eventual separation from Jugga symbolizing how personal relationships are destroyed by communal boundaries imposed overnight [1, p. 1]. Magistrate Hukum Chand, the district official responsible for maintaining order, represents the moral paralysis of authority during the crisis. Though personally troubled by the violence unfolding around him, he remains largely ineffective, his guilt-ridden conscience reflecting the failure of the state to protect its citizens [1, p. 1]. Singh employs irony to expose this failure, as Hukum Chand's liaison with a Muslim girl named Haseena—a relationship that mirrors Jugga and Nooran's in its transgression of communal boundaries—renders him morally compromised and unable to act decisively [3, p. 6]. The character of Iqbal Singh, a communist agitator from the city, introduces an ideological dimension to the narrative, criticizing both the Congress government and the feudal structures that perpetuate inequality [8, p. 2]. Through these diverse characters, Singh demonstrates that Partition violence cannot be attributed to any single type of person but rather emerges from the complex interplay of fear, ideology, and institutional failure.

The theme of communal violence in *Train to Pakistan* is developed through a carefully orchestrated progression from harmony to suspicion to outright brutality. The first sign of rupture arrives with the ghost train from Pakistan, carrying the corpses of massacred Hindus and Sikhs. Singh's description of this moment is deliberately restrained yet profoundly impactful: "Everyone in Mano Majra knew by then that the train was carrying the dead" [4, p. 1]. The train, once a symbol of connection and movement, becomes a harbinger of death, transforming abstract rumors of violence into undeniable reality. This event instills fear and suspicion in the villagers, eroding the trust that had sustained communal relations for generations [4, p. 1]. Singh captures the psychological shift with precision: "The fact was that the Muslims were afraid. The Sikhs were afraid too" [4, p. 1]. Fear, rather than hatred, emerges as the primary emotion driving human action, replacing empathy and reason with a desperate need for security. The novel demonstrates that violence in such contexts is less an expression of innate cruelty than a response to perceived threat, a distinction that complicates any simple moral judgment of the perpetrators.

One of the novel's most disturbing achievements is its portrayal of how ordinary people become complicit in violence through the gradual erosion of moral boundaries. **1.4 Research Methodology and Scope**

The present study undertakes a comparative textual analysis of Khushwant Singh’s *Train to Pakistan* within the intersecting frameworks of historical inquiry and literary criticism, with the explicit aim of examining the representation of Partition violence and the articulation of humanism in the novel. This methodological approach recognizes that literary texts do not merely reflect historical events but actively shape, contest, and complicate collective memory. As Gyanendra Pandey argues, partition literature offers a crucial “defense of the fragment,” providing access to subaltern experiences that official nationalist historiography tends to elide or subsume under grand narratives of national liberation [2, p. 27-55]. Consequently, the methodology adopted here proceeds from the premise that a rigorous analysis of Singh’s novel requires sustained attention to both its literary form and its historical embeddedness, acknowledging that the aesthetic choices made by the author—characterization, narrative structure, imagery, and tone—are inseparable from the ideological and ethical positions the text advances.

The primary method employed is close comparative textual analysis, focusing on the linguistic, symbolic, and structural dimensions of *Train to Pakistan* in relation to other partition narratives and historiographical accounts. This method draws upon narratological frameworks developed by theorists such as Jean-Michel Adam and Gérard Genette, who emphasize the importance of analyzing how narrative discourse organizes, selects, and evaluates events [1]. In the context of partition fiction, attention to narrative perspective becomes particularly significant, as the choice of focalization—whether through the eyes of a Sikh, Muslim, or Hindu character; a victim, a perpetrator, or a bystander—directly shapes the reader’s affective and moral engagement with the violence depicted. Singh’s decision to center the narrative on the village of Mano Majra, with its intimate, localized perspective on events of national magnitude, constitutes a deliberate aesthetic and political strategy that the present analysis seeks to interrogate [3, p. 1].

Furthermore, the methodology incorporates a historicist approach that situates Singh’s novel within the broader discursive field of partition historiography. This entails reading *Train to Pakistan* alongside both mainstream nationalist histories and revisionist accounts that foreground subaltern and gendered experiences of violence. Scholars such as Urvashi Butalia and Ritu Menon have demonstrated that official histories of Partition systematically marginalized the voices of women, Dalits, and other marginalized communities [1]. By bringing Singh’s text into dialogue with these historiographical interventions, the present study aims to assess the extent to which the novel either reinforces or challenges hegemonic narratives of the event. Importantly, this comparative framework allows for an examination of what Dipak Raj Joshi terms the “affective politics” of partition literature—the ways in which texts mobilize emotions such as fear, hatred, pity, and love to construct communities of sympathy and to assign moral responsibility [9, p. 1]. As Joshi contends, Singh’s claim to objectivity and detachment must be scrutinized against the text’s actual rhetorical operations, which may betray a partisan investment in protecting Sikh identity from accusations of brutality [8, p. 2].

The scope of the study is deliberately focused, concentrating primarily on *Train to Pakistan* while drawing selectively on other partition novels, particularly Bhisham Sahni’s *Tamas*, for comparative illumination. This focus is justified on several grounds. *Train to Pakistan* occupies a foundational position in the corpus of Indian English partition fiction, being the first novel ever written on the theme of partition and partition violence [9, p. 33]. Its canonical status means that subsequent partition narratives have, to varying degrees, engaged with, contested, or replicated its representational strategies. Moreover, the novel’s setting in a single village—Mano Majra—offers a microcosmic lens through which the macro-level forces of communal violence can be examined with particular intensity and nuance. As critics have observed, Singh’s decision to limit the geographical and social scope of the narrative enables a more profound exploration of how ordinary individuals navigate, resist, or capitulate to the demands of collective hatred [3, p. 1].

Despite these strengths, the study acknowledges several important limitations. First, the analysis is restricted to the English-language text of *Train to Pakistan*, and does not examine translations of the novel into other Indian languages or the reception of the work in non-English reading communities. Given that the readership of English-language fiction in India during the 1940s and 1950s constituted no more than three percent of the total population, the novel’s audience was predominantly urban, educated, and elite [10, p. 8]. This demographic reality raises questions about the representativeness of the perspectives offered in the text and about the political implications of writing about subaltern suffering in a language inaccessible to most of the actual victims. The present study engages with this limitation by situating the novel’s reception within the broader socio-linguistic context of postcolonial India, but it does not attempt a reception study or an ethnography of readership.

Second, the analysis focuses primarily on the novel’s depiction of violence and humanism, and does not provide exhaustive treatment of other thematic concerns such as gender, caste, or the role of the state, except insofar as these intersect with the central argument. While the representation of women in *Train to Pakistan*—particularly figures such as Nooran—is addressed in relation to the gendered dimensions of communal violence, a fuller feminist analysis would require sustained attention to the novel’s silences and omissions regarding women’s agency and resistance [4, p. 1]. Similarly, the role of the state, embodied in the morally paralyzed figure of Magistrate Hukum Chand, is analyzed as a thematic element but not subjected to a comprehensive political-theoretical examination. These delimitations are necessary to maintain analytical focus and coherence within the constraints of the present study.

Third, the study engages with existing criticism on *Train to Pakistan* but does not claim to offer an exhaustive survey of the scholarship. Particular attention is paid to readings that foreground the novel’s relationship to nationalist historiography and to the politics of affect, as articulated by critics such as Rituparna Roy, Peter Morey, and Beerendra Pandey [2]. However, the study does not systematically address psychoanalytic interpretations, postcolonial theoretical frameworks in their full complexity, or comparative analyses with non-Indian partition literatures, such as those emerging from the 1947 division of Palestine. These omissions are acknowledged as areas for potential future research rather than as deficits in the present argument.

The textual evidence analyzed in this study is drawn from the 2002 Penguin edition of *Train to Pakistan*, which is included in the *Memories of Madness* anthology [1]. In addition to the novel itself, the analysis draws upon Singh’s interviews and critical commentary, as well as historical sources that document the events of Partition. The use of multiple types of evidence—literary, historical, and critical—is intended to triangulate the analysis and to guard against the hermeneutic circularity that can result from reading a text solely within its own terms. As scholars of partition have warned, the line between historical documentation and literary representation is often blurred, and this blurring itself becomes a subject of critical inquiry [2, p. 27-55].

Finally, the study acknowledges the ethical complexities inherent in writing about violence and trauma. The analysis proceeds with a sensitivity to the suffering of all communities affected by Partition, and it does not seek to assign blame to one group over another. Rather, the aim is to understand how literary texts construct, negotiate, and sometimes contest the meaning of violence. In this regard, the study aligns with the humanistic commitment that informs Singh’s own novel—a commitment to affirming the dignity of individual life even in the face of collective madness [3, p. 1]. The transition to the following section, which will undertake a detailed chapter-by-chapter analysis of *Train to Pakistan*, is thus guided by the methodological principles outlined here: a rigorous attention to textual detail, a historicist awareness of the contexts of production and reception, and a critical sensitivity to the affective and political dimensions of literary representation.

**1.5 Thesis Statement**

This dissertation argues that Khushwant Singh’s *Train to Pakistan* (1956) simultaneously subverts and reinforces dominant nationalist narratives of the Partition of India by deploying a complex affective politics that oscillates between exposing the catastrophic breakdown of humanist values and reproducing communal biases through the selective attribution of violence and moral responsibility. While the novel has been widely celebrated as a foundational text that reveals the horrors of a forgotten past with realism and bitterness [5, p. 1], a closer examination of its narrative strategies, character construction, and affective investments reveals a more ambivalent relationship to the very mainstream representations it purports to challenge.

The central contention of this study is that *Train to Pakistan* operates through a double movement: on one hand, it undermines official histories that silence Partition violence through grand narratives of national liberation; on the other hand, it participates in what might be termed a politics of affective differentiation that constructs "love groups" and "hate groups" along communal lines [9, p. 14]. This argument builds upon the recognition that Partition fiction has been understood as "almost the only social history we have of this time," offering an attempt "to assimilate the full import of what Partition meant" that other genres fail to provide [5, p. 1]. However, this dissertation contends that such claims to historiographical authenticity must be interrogated through an analysis of the novel's own ideological investments and affective manipulations.

The dissertation advances this argument through four interconnected claims. First, Singh's novel exposes the catastrophic failure of state institutions and political leadership during Partition, thereby challenging the triumphalist narrative of independence that dominated postcolonial Indian discourse. The character of Magistrate Hukum Chand embodies this failure: though personally troubled by the violence, he remains "largely ineffective," and his "guilt-ridden conscience reflects the moral paralysis of authority during Partition" [1, p. 1]. Singh uses irony to expose this failure, as when Chand reflects that "the machinery of the state had collapsed," creating an administrative vacuum that "enables communal violence to flourish unchecked" [1, p. 1]. More pointedly, the novel satirizes Jawaharlal Nehru's famous "tryst with destiny" speech, with Chand questioning the idea of national experience by contrasting the ceremonial participation of elites with the horrific deaths of ordinary people: "Yes, Mr Prime Minister, you made your tryst, so did many others" [5, p. 1]. This subversive dimension of the novel constitutes what might be termed a "sub-version" of national history, effecting "a change in the scale and the subject of history" that transforms "the glorious hypotext of independence" into "an outraged hypertext of Partition" [4, p. 2].

Second, the novel demonstrates how ordinary people become complicit in communal violence through the mechanisms of collective fear, revenge, and the normalization of hatred. Singh does not portray the Sikhs of Mano Majra as inherently cruel; rather, he shows how "collective fear and revenge override moral judgment" [1, p. 1]. This is evident when plans are made to attack the train carrying Muslims to Pakistan: "They were not bad men. They were not murderers. But they were afraid" [1, p. 1]. Violence here is "normalized, even rationalized, as a defensive necessity" [1, p. 1]. This portrayal aligns with theoretical understandings of how groups develop "heightened affectivity and lower level of intellectual functioning," leading to the formation of "herd instinct" and the automatic differentiation between "us" and "them" [7, p. 11]. The novel thus demonstrates how communal boundaries are imposed overnight, destroying personal relationships that had previously transcended religious identity, as symbolized by Nooran's "silent terror and eventual separation from Jugga" [1, p. 1].

Third, the dissertation argues that despite its apparent evenhandedness, *Train to Pakistan* engages in a politics of affective investment that constructs negative affect toward Pakistani Muslims while cultivating positive affect toward secular India. This claim challenges the widespread critical consensus that Singh "refuses to assign blame to one community over another, emphasizing instead the shared suffering of Sikhs, Muslims, and Hindus" [1, p. 1]. A careful reading reveals that Singh's narrative is "tilted to his community" and that his "biasness against (Pakistani) Muslims is psychologically propelled, as reaction formation of love towards secular India" [10, p. 6]. The novel invests considerable effort in forming a "conscience collective" about Pakistani Muslims, ultimately contributing to a "nationalist mainstream narrative based on hatred to the other" [10, p. 6]. This is evident in passages where Hukum Chand condemns Pakistani authorities for their brutality while simultaneously engaging in morally questionable behavior himself, creating an ironic discrepancy that the novel does not fully resolve [10, p. 6].

Fourth, the dissertation contends that Singh's representation of gendered violence and the figure of the sacrificial hero complicates but does not entirely transcend the novel's communal biases. The novel highlights how women's bodies become "sites of revenge and humiliation" during Partition, with the "forced evacuation of Muslim women and children from Mano Majra" underscoring "the disproportionate suffering borne by women" [1, p. 1]. However, the treatment of female characters remains largely instrumental to the novel's broader affective project. Similarly, Jugga's final act—cutting the rope meant to derail the train carrying Muslims to Pakistan—is presented as a moment of moral redemption that "asserts that humanity can survive even in the darkest times, though at a terrible cost" [1, p. 1]. Yet this individual act of sacrifice, motivated by love for Nooran, does not fundamentally challenge the communal logic that structures the novel's world. Jugga's death remains "impulsive, emotional, and deeply human" [1, p. 1], but it is also contained within a narrative framework that has already established the moral culpability of the other community.

The dissertation thus intervenes in ongoing scholarly debates about the relationship between literature, history, and memory in the context of Partition. While critics such as Rituparna Roy have positioned *Train to Pakistan* as a foundational text that "reveals the horror of a forgotten past" [5, p. 1], this study argues that the novel's relationship to forgetting is more complex. Drawing on the distinction between two types of forgetting—silence and self-exoneration—the dissertation demonstrates that *Train to Pakistan* "both undermines and upholds mainstream representations of the Partition" [5, p. 1]. The novel's subversive potential lies in its refusal to countenance the silencing of victims, as when it substitutes national history with "a series of individual stories" that invert the meaning of Nehru's speech [4, p. 2]. However, its simultaneous participation in affective politics that attribute violence disproportionately to one community reveals the limits of its critique.

Methodologically, this study employs an affective reading strategy that attends to the emotional investments and ideological work performed by the novel's narrative techniques. Drawing on theoretical frameworks from affect theory, including Sara Ahmed's concept of the "cultural politics of emotion" and the formation of "love groups" and "hate groups" [9, p. 14], the dissertation analyzes how Singh's text produces and circulates affect in ways that both challenge and reinforce communal boundaries. This approach allows for a nuanced understanding of how Partition literature functions as a site of memory and contestation, while also acknowledging the political and psychological pressures that shape literary representations of traumatic historical events.

In making this argument, the dissertation contributes to the broader field of Partition studies by demonstrating the need for critical readings that move beyond celebratory accounts of literary humanism. The novel's famous concluding image—"His body went limp, but the train went safely on to Pakistan" [1, p. 1]—has been read as affirming the possibility of human compassion surviving even in the darkest times. This dissertation does not deny the power of this moment, but it insists that such readings must be contextualized within the novel's larger affective economy, one that simultaneously constructs the Muslim other as the primary agent of violence while reserving moral redemption for the Hindu-Sikh protagonist. The dissertation thus argues that *Train to Pakistan* exemplifies the ambivalent position of Partition literature, which must negotiate between the imperative to remember and the psychological need to construct narratives that make suffering meaningful within particular communal and national frameworks.

The following chapters will develop this argument through a sustained analysis of the novel's narrative strategies, character construction, and affective investments. Chapter Two examines the novel's representation of state failure and the critique of nationalist historiography, analyzing how Singh's subversive portrayal of political authority challenges official narratives of independence. Chapter Three turns to the mechanisms of communal violence, exploring how the novel depicts the transformation of ordinary villagers into perpetrators of atrocity through the dynamics of collective fear and affective contagion. Chapter Four analyzes the gendered dimensions of Partition violence in the novel, examining how women's bodies function as sites of both suffering and symbolic meaning within the text's affective economy. Chapter Five considers the figure of the sacrificial hero and the possibilities of moral redemption, interrogating whether Jugga's final act represents a genuine transcendence of communal logic or a containment of the novel's more radical implications. The conclusion reflects on the broader implications of this analysis for understanding the relationship between literature, memory, and the politics of affect in the context of collective trauma.
