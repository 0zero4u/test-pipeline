### Abstract (150–250 words)

This dissertation examines the representation of Partition violence and humanism in Khushwant Singh's novel *Train to Pakistan* (1956) and its film adaptation. Through comparative textual and cinematic analysis, the study investigates how both the novel and film portray the catastrophic breakdown of communal harmony during the 1947 Partition of India. The research draws upon primary sources including the novel, film, and critical scholarship on Partition literature and film adaptation theory.

The study reveals that Singh's novel simultaneously subverts and reinforces dominant nationalist narratives through a complex affective politics that oscillates between exposing institutional failure and reproducing communal biases. The film adaptation transforms these literary strategies through visual representation, altering the emphasis on violence, humanism, and moral conflict.

Key findings demonstrate that both texts operate within a tension between collective trauma and individual redemption, with the novel emphasizing psychological interiority and the film foregrounding visual spectacle. The comparative analysis reveals how adaptation processes reshape the representation of Partition violence while maintaining certain ideological commitments to particular communal perspectives.

This research contributes to Partition studies by demonstrating the need for critical readings that move beyond celebratory accounts of literary humanism, attending instead to the political and psychological pressures that shape representations of collective trauma.

## AI WRITING GUIDELINES

### What AI Should Do

1. **Citation-Grounded**
   - Every claim must cite a source `[N, p. X]`
   - No unsupported statements
   - Direct quotes only when necessary

2. **Academic Tone**
   - Formal, objective, third-person
   - No contractions, no "you", no slang
   - Use active voice (passive in methods only)

3. **Comparative Analysis**
   - Always compare novel vs. film
   - Highlight differences and similarities
   - Use specific examples from both texts

4. **Section Transitions**
   - Each section ends with transition to next
   - Chapter ends with preview of next chapter
   - Logical flow between paragraphs

5. **Evidence Integration**
   - Paraphrase more than quote
   - Integrate evidence smoothly
   - Analyze, don't summarize

### What AI Should NOT Do

1. **No Hallucination**
   - Do not invent quotes or citations
   - Do not fabricate statistics
   - Do not invent character names or plot points

2. **No Repetition**
   - Avoid repeating the same point
   - Vary sentence structure
   - Use different transition words

3. **No Filler**
   - No "In conclusion"
   - No "It is important to note"
   - No "The future looks bright"

4. **No First-Person**
   - No "I think"
   - No "In my opinion"
   - Use "The evidence suggests"

### Citation Format (MLA 9th Edition)

**In-text:**
- Single: `[1]`, `[2]`
- With page: `[1, p. 45]`, `[2, pp. 10-15]`
- Multiple: `[1, 2, 3]`
- Range: `[1-3]`

**Works Cited:**
```
Author Last, First. "Title." Journal, vol. X, no. Y, Year, pp. X-Y.
Author Last, First. *Title*. Publisher, Year.
```

## WRITING SEQUENCE

Write chapters in this order (each builds on previous):

```
1. Title Page + Abstract
   ↓
2. Chapter I (Introduction)
   ↓
3. Chapter II (Literature Review)
   ↓
4. Chapter III (Partition and Violence)
   ↓
5. Chapter IV (Humanism and Moral Conflict)
   ↓
6. Chapter V (Adaptation Analysis)
   ↓
7. Conclusion
   ↓
8. Works Cited (compiled from all chapters)
```

**Why sequential:**
- Each chapter references previous chapters
- DissertationState tracks cross-chapter context
- Conclusion synthesizes all chapters
- Works Cited accumulates all citations
