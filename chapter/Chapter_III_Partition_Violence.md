# Chapter III

# Partition and Violence in Train to Pakistan

**3.1 Historical Violence in the Novel**

Khushwant Singh’s *Train to Pakistan* (1956) constitutes a seminal literary intervention into the historiography of the 1947 Partition, offering a sustained and unflinching examination of communal violence as both a historical catastrophe and a deeply personal tragedy. The novel situates its narrative within the microcosm of Mano Majra, a border village whose harmonious coexistence of Sikhs and Muslims becomes progressively corroded by the forces of political manipulation, administrative failure, and collective fear. Rather than presenting violence as an inexplicable eruption of primordial hatred, Singh meticulously traces its social, political, and psychological determinants, thereby constructing a complex argument about the conditions under which ordinary individuals become complicit in atrocity. This section examines the representation of historical violence in the novel, analyzing its political dimensions, its gendered manifestations, and its depiction of communal complicity, before comparing Singh’s literary treatment with the cinematic adaptation directed by Pamela Rooks.

The political architecture of Partition violence, as represented in the novel, emerges not from spontaneous communal animosity but from a calculated manipulation of local populations by external actors. Singh portrays the state apparatus as fundamentally compromised, with field-level administrators actively abetting or passively condoning violence when it aligned with their communal interests. The inspector’s declaration that “our RSS boys beat up Muslim gangs in all cities” and his accusation that Sikhs “have lost their manliness” by failing to participate reveals how law enforcement personnel functioned as partisan actors rather than neutral arbiters [1, pp. 19-20]. This institutional failure is compounded by what critics have identified as the “distance and dissonance between elite, secular nationalist politics and the alienated, resentful actions in the state apparatus entrusted to translate that secular national vision into reality” [1, p. 3]. The novel thus exposes a fundamental rupture between the cosmopolitan secularism of national leadership and the parochial communal loyalties that governed local administration. Furthermore, Singh demonstrates how the chaotic conditions of Partition created opportunities for criminal elements to exploit the breakdown of order. The character of Mali and his gang exemplify how violence functioned not merely as an aftermath of political division but as a deliberate tool to enforce demographic transformation, sending a message to minorities that they “must leave or accept brutal punishment” [1, p. 3]. This instrumentalization of violence reveals Partition as a process actively produced through terror rather than a spontaneous conflagration.

The social dynamics of violence in Mano Majra are portrayed with particular attention to the mechanisms through which peaceful communities are transformed into murderous mobs. Singh depicts the arrival of outsiders who systematically undermine the village’s longstanding communal harmony through emotional blackmail and appeals to masculine potency. The agitators’ demand that villagers match atrocities committed against their co-religionists—“For each Hindu or Sikh, they kill, kill two Mussulmans”—constitutes a rhetoric of reciprocal violence that erodes moral restraint [1, p. 149]. The novel’s most disturbing revelation is the speed with which ordinary villagers become willing to butcher neighbors with whom they have “lived for generations in a peaceful cohabitation” and for whose departure they had “only recently wept” [1, pp. 151-152]. This transformation is not presented as evidence of innate human cruelty but as a consequence of systematic psychological manipulation. The agitators succeed because they exploit the villagers’ fear and their sense of emasculation, asking pointed questions about their potency to “emotionally blackmail them into violence” [1, p. 149]. Singh’s analysis thus aligns with broader scholarly observations that Partition violence was often “guided by economic interest” and that “religion is no consideration among buyers and sellers” when profit becomes the overwhelming motif [5, p. 40]. The novel refuses to romanticize the villagers as innocent victims, instead presenting their gradual descent into complicity as a cautionary tale about the fragility of communal bonds under conditions of manufactured fear.

The gendered dimensions of Partition violence receive sustained attention in the novel, though Singh’s treatment is characterized by restraint rather than graphic sensationalism. Women’s bodies become sites of communal revenge and humiliation, with reports of women “jumping into wells and burning themselves rather than falling into the hands of Muslims” and those who did not commit suicide being “paraded naked in the streets, raped in public, and then murdered” [8, p. 121]. These accounts, relayed through refugee testimony, establish a pattern of sexual violence that transcends communal boundaries. Singh ensures that the reader understands the symmetrical nature of this brutality, describing how Muslim women similarly endured the removal of their veils, being “stripped and marched down crowded streets to be raped in the marketplace,” and how many “eluded their would-be ravishers by killing themselves” [8, p. 120]. The novel’s representation of gendered violence thus refuses to assign exclusive blame to one community, instead emphasizing the universal vulnerability of women during communal conflict. The character of Nooran embodies this gendered precarity; her silent terror and eventual separation from Jugga symbolize how personal relationships are destroyed by communal boundaries imposed overnight [12, p. 1]. Singh’s decision to foreground the threat of sexual violence without depicting it graphically serves a strategic purpose: it forces readers to confront the horror without the potential voyeurism of explicit representation, maintaining the novel’s documentary tone while acknowledging the unspeakable.

The graphic picturization of violence in the novel extends beyond gendered brutality to encompass the sheer scale and horror of mass death. Singh employs vivid sensory imagery to convey the transformation of the landscape into a site of atrocity. The description of the northern horizon turning “orange” and then “copper” and “luminous russet” before “red tongues of flame leapt into the black sky” creates a visual spectacle of destruction that is immediately undercut by the olfactory horror of “the smell of burning kerosene, then of wood. And then—a faint acrid smell of searing flesh” [8, p. 84]. This progression from the aesthetic to the visceral forces readers to confront the material reality of mass cremation. Similarly, the description of dead bodies floating in the river—“some were without limbs, some had their bellies torn open, and many women’s breasts were slashed”—presents a tableau of systematic mutilation that exceeds individual acts of violence [8, pp. 143-144]. The presence of “kites and vultures” overhead reinforces the sense of nature itself participating in the degradation of human dignity. Singh’s treatment of these scenes has been characterized as possessing “artistic objectivity” in which he “exaggerates nothing, he leaves nothing” [8, p. 162]. This documentary precision serves to authenticate the historical record while simultaneously generating an affective response that mere statistics could not produce.

The novel’s representation of violence is distinguished by its refusal to adopt a one-sided perspective, instead presenting the suffering of all communities with equal gravity. Singh portrays the emotional and psychological trauma experienced by Muslims in Mano Majra as they receive news of massacres in Sikh-majority areas, creating an “air of uncertainty” that pervades the village [8, p. 120]. The arrival of a second train full of dead bodies, which the villagers expect will be cremated with the same ritual attention as the first, only to have “a bulldozer arrived from somewhere” to dispose of the Muslim corpses, underscores the differential value assigned to lives across communal lines [8, p. 145]. This moment crystallizes the novel’s critique of how violence dehumanizes not only its victims but also its perpetrators, reducing the dead to waste requiring mechanical disposal. Magistrate Hukum Chand’s horrified exclamation—“Harey Ram, Harey Ram. Fifteen hundred innocent people! What else is a Kalyug?”—captures the moral paralysis of authority figures who witness atrocity without the capacity to intervene [8, pp. 96-97]. The novel thus presents violence as a systemic breakdown that implicates all levels of society, from the political leadership to the local administrator to the ordinary villager.

A comparative analysis of the novel and its 1998 film adaptation directed by Pamela Rooks reveals significant differences in the representation of historical violence, differences that reflect the constraints and possibilities of each medium. Both the novel and the film maintain fidelity to the central theme of communal harmony destroyed by Partition, and both employ the arrival of the “ghost train” carrying massacred bodies as a pivotal symbol of the violence that disrupts idyllic village life [4, p. 1]. The film preserves key events including the murder of Lala Ramlal, the dacoity committed by Mali’s gang, and Jugga’s tragic sacrifice to prevent the train from being blown up [4, p. 1]. However, the film necessarily condenses the novel’s expansive treatment of time and character, focusing on the immediate sense of urgency and tension rather than the deeper exploration of character motivations present in the literary text [4, p. 1]. This condensation results in the loss of certain character nuances, particularly Iqbal’s political ideology and Jugga’s backstory, which in the novel provide crucial context for understanding how individuals become drawn into or resist the logic of communal violence [7, p. 2].

The most significant divergence between novel and film lies in their respective treatments of character complexity and moral ambiguity. The novel presents Iqbal as a “cynical intellectual who questions the efficacy of political solutions and grapples with the limitations of his own idealism,” whereas the film portrays him as a “more straightforward idealist, driven by a desire to bring about social change” [7, p. 2]. Similarly, Hukumchand’s character in the novel is marked by internal moral conflict and awareness of the “injustice being inflicted upon the Muslim population,” while the film simplifies him into a “helpless bureaucrat” [7, p. 2]. These adaptations reflect the cinematic medium’s need to streamline complex narratives, but they also diminish the novel’s sustained critique of how even well-intentioned individuals become complicit in violence through inaction. The film’s greater emphasis on action and suspense surrounding the climax, focusing on the “physical and emotional intensity of the final moments,” contrasts with the novel’s slower buildup that “allows the reader to reflect on the larger political and social implications of the events” [7, p. 2]. The novel’s use of descriptive passages to capture the essence of village life, replaced in the film by the musical element of “Sanu Aa Milya Yaar Piyaariya,” represents a fundamental difference in how each medium constructs meaning: the novel relies on linguistic precision to generate reflection, while the film employs affective immediacy to produce emotional engagement [7, p. 2].

The novel’s representation of historical violence thus constitutes a sophisticated argument about the conditions that enable atrocity, an argument that the film adaptation necessarily simplifies for cinematic purposes. Singh’s refusal to assign blame to any single community, his exposure of administrative complicity, his attention to gendered violence, and his insistence on the humanity of all victims combine to produce a text that functions simultaneously as historical document, moral critique, and literary achievement. The novel demonstrates that Partition violence was not an inevitable consequence of religious difference but a manufactured catastrophe produced by political manipulation, administrative failure, and the exploitation of collective fear. This analysis of historical violence provides the foundation for examining the novel’s treatment of trauma and its psychological aftermath, to which the following section turns.

# 3.2 Communal Breakdown in Mano Majra

The dissolution of communal harmony in Mano Majra constitutes one of the most significant thematic developments in Khushwant Singh's *Train to Pakistan*, representing the microcosmic collapse of interfaith relations that characterized the broader Partition catastrophe. Both the novel and its film adaptation by Pamela Brooks and Nirmal Pandey trace the trajectory from peaceful coexistence to violent fragmentation, yet each medium employs distinct narrative strategies to convey the psychological and social mechanisms through which communal bonds disintegrate. The comparative analysis of these portrayals reveals how Singh's literary text and its cinematic counterpart negotiate the complex interplay between external political forces and internal communal dynamics that ultimately destroy the village's social fabric.

The initial depiction of Mano Majra in both the novel and the film establishes a foundation of interfaith harmony that renders the subsequent breakdown all the more devastating. Singh describes the village as a place where "they had always been Hindus and Muslims, Sikhs and Christians, and they had always been friends," emphasizing that communal identities were imposed from outside rather than emerging from internal divisions [8, p. 8]. The novel presents a meticulously detailed portrait of daily coexistence: the Sikh temple and the mosque stand as the only brick buildings alongside the moneylender's home, symbolizing the spatial integration of religious communities [6, p. 2]. The villagers share a common deity—a three-foot sandstone slab under a keekar tree—to which "all the villagers—Hindu, Sikh, Muslim or pseudo-Christian—repair secretly whenever they are in a special need of blessing" [11, p. 8]. This syncretic practice demonstrates that religious boundaries, while acknowledged, did not preclude shared spiritual life. The film adaptation faithfully reproduces this atmosphere of harmony, creating "an atmosphere of love, harmony, and peace among all the people regardless of their castes, affiliations and doctrines" [6, p. 2]. Both versions thus establish Mano Majra as a symbolic representation of pre-Partition India, where communal identities coexisted within a framework of mutual respect and economic interdependence.

The arrival of the ghost train carrying massacred bodies functions as the primary catalyst for communal breakdown in both the novel and the film, transforming the village's relationship with its Muslim inhabitants. Singh describes how the train loaded with corpses of Sikhs and Hindus creates "a commotion in Mano Majra," with people standing on their roofs to witness the horrifying spectacle [12, p. 2]. The novel emphasizes the psychological impact of this event: "That evening, for the first time in the memory of Mano Majra, Imam Baksh's resonant cry did not ascend to the heavens to declare the glory of God" [13, p. 1]. The silence of the muezzin's call signifies the rupture of the religious rhythm that had structured village life. The film adaptation preserves this pivotal moment, with Rooks emphasizing "the brutal realities of Partition" through scenes such as "the close-up of Imam Baksh's fearful expression as he anticipates the train," which "captures the horror and helplessness of the situation" [9, p. 1]. Both versions thus employ the ghost train as a symbol that transforms from representing British imperialism to embodying "the carnage and tragedy caused by the Partition" [9, p. 1]. The visual medium of film, however, intensifies the visceral impact of this transformation through its capacity to depict the mutilated bodies directly, whereas the novel relies on descriptive language to evoke horror.

The process of communal polarization unfolds through the spread of rumors and the manipulation of fear, mechanisms that both the novel and the film depict with careful attention to psychological detail. Singh demonstrates how "rumours about Muslims being slaughtered in Patiala, Ambala and Amritsar, mosques being demolished and the holy Koran being torn by infidels" circulate through the village, creating an atmosphere of mutual suspicion [7, p. 1]. The novel presents the symmetrical structure of communal accusation: "Muslims brood over the rumours of atrocities compiled by Sikh. They have heard of gentlewomen having their veils taken off, being stripped and marched down crowded streets to be raped in the market place" while Sikhs respond with the conviction that "never trust a Musalman" [7, p. 1]. This reciprocal fear-mongering demonstrates how communalism operates through psychological mechanisms rather than direct experience. The film adaptation condenses this process, focusing "more on the central conflict and the immediate sense of urgency and tension surrounding the Partition" [9, p. 1]. While the novel can explore the gradual erosion of trust through extended narrative development, the film must compress this transformation into visual and auditory cues—the changing expressions of characters, the altered tone of conversations, the physical separation of groups within the village space.

The role of external agitators in accelerating communal breakdown receives significant attention in both versions, though the novel provides more extensive treatment of the ideological mechanisms at work. Singh introduces young Sikh refugees who arrive in Mano Majra and challenge the villagers' masculinity and religious commitment: "Is this a Sikh village? ... What sort of Sikh are you? Potent or impotent?" [10, p. 2]. These outsiders employ what one critic terms "affective logic," using emotional appeals to ancestral glory and religious pride to mobilize the villagers toward violence [10, p. 2]. The novel describes how "the young refugees mould the Mano Majra Sikhs," transforming peaceful peasants into potential killers through rhetorical manipulation [10, p. 2]. The film adaptation retains this element, showing how "the newcomers ask questions about their potency to emotionally blackmail them into violence" [14, p. 3]. Both versions demonstrate that communal violence does not emerge spontaneously from pre-existing hatred but is deliberately manufactured by interested parties who exploit vulnerabilities in communal identity. The novel, however, provides more nuanced treatment of the ideological diversity among the agitators, including the socialist character Iqbal who represents an alternative political vision that ultimately fails to counter the communalist tide.

The failure of institutional authority to prevent communal breakdown constitutes a central critique in both the novel and the film, with the character of Hukum Chand embodying the moral weakness of political leadership. Singh portrays the magistrate as a figure who "understands the tragedy of Partition" yet "remains passive and ineffective," suggesting that "political ambition and administrative failure played a major role in communal violence" [3, p. 1]. The novel reveals Hukum Chand's hypocrisy through his "dirty liaison with a Muslim girl, Haseena, almost his daughter's age," even as he facilitates the evacuation of Muslims from the village [15, p. 6]. This contradiction exposes the gap between personal desire and political action, between individual humanity and institutional complicity. The film adaptation preserves Hukum Chand's character as "the District Commissioner" who "struggles to maintain order and make decisions that align with his own beliefs while adhering to the political pressures of the time" [8, p. 8]. Both versions thus critique the administrative apparatus that failed to protect citizens during the Partition, though the novel provides more extensive exploration of Hukum Chand's internal conflicts and the systemic corruption that enables communal violence.

The transformation of ordinary villagers into participants in communal violence represents the most disturbing aspect of the breakdown, and both the novel and the film trace this metamorphosis with careful attention to its moral implications. Singh describes how "the same villagers including the refugees became ready to attack Muslims including their sisters," noting that "some villagers who had only recently wept at the departure of their Muslim friends also stood up to volunteer" [11, p. 8]. This paradox—that those who mourned the departure of Muslim neighbors could simultaneously prepare to murder them—reveals the profound psychological dislocation produced by communal propaganda. The novel emphasizes that "the partition caused violence and it further led to displacement, dismemberment, and feeling of trauma among the people of both religions" [11, p. 8]. The film adaptation captures this transformation through visual means, showing how "the peace-loving peasants turn into adversaries when influenced by the reverberations of partition" [13, p. 1]. Both versions thus demonstrate that communal violence represents not the expression of authentic communal identity but its pathological distortion under conditions of fear and manipulation.

The love story of Jugga and Nooran provides a counterpoint to the narrative of communal breakdown, representing the possibility of transcendence that both the novel and the film preserve as a central thematic element. Singh presents their relationship as one that "challenges communal boundaries and demonstrates the power of personal affection over religious hatred" [1, p. 2]. Jugga, a Sikh dacoit, and Nooran, a Muslim weaver's daughter, embody the potential for human connection to overcome the divisions that tear the village apart. The novel traces Jugga's transformation from a "rough village youth" to a "moral hero" whose sacrifice to save the train carrying Muslim refugees—including Nooran—represents "redemption and the triumph of humanity over communal hatred" [1, p. 2]. The film adaptation preserves this narrative arc, with "Jugga's tragic sacrifice to prevent the train from being blown up, saving Nooran and others in the process" serving as the climactic moment [9, p. 1]. Both versions thus employ the love story as a narrative device that affirms the persistence of human values even in the midst of communal catastrophe. The film, however, necessarily condenses the development of this relationship, losing "certain character nuances and subplots, including Iqbal's political ideology and Jugga's past" [9, p. 1].

The comparative analysis of the novel and film adaptation reveals significant differences in their treatment of communal breakdown, particularly regarding the representation of violence and the complexity of character motivation. The novel provides "a more expansive treatment of time and characters," delving "deeply into the backstories and motivations of the characters, particularly Jugga and Iqbal" [9, p. 1]. This extended narrative allows Singh to explore the gradual psychological process through which communal suspicion develops, tracing the accumulation of rumors, the impact of external events, and the erosion of trust over time. The film, by contrast, "condenses the timeline, focusing more on the central conflict and the immediate sense of urgency and tension surrounding the Partition" [9, p. 1]. This condensation necessarily simplifies the causal chain that leads to communal violence, potentially obscuring the complex social and political factors that the novel examines in detail. Furthermore, the novel's treatment of violence is more ambivalent, acknowledging that "both sides killed. Both shot and stabbed and speared and clubbed. Both tortured. Both raped" [2, p. 2], whereas the film, according to some critics, may emphasize atrocities committed by one side more than the other [10, p. 2].

The representation of communal breakdown in both the novel and the film ultimately serves to critique the political and social forces that destroyed interfaith harmony in the subcontinent. Singh's novel demonstrates that "communalism is shown as a destructive force that destroys social harmony and human relationships," operating through "fear, revenge, and misinformation" [3, p. 1]. The film adaptation, while necessarily compressing this analysis, preserves the essential critique of how ordinary people become complicit in violence through manipulation and fear. Both versions affirm that "genuine human connections are stronger than communal identities," as demonstrated through Jugga's sacrifice and the persistence of love across religious boundaries [1, p. 2]. The transition to the subsequent section will examine the representation of trauma and displacement that follows the communal breakdown, analyzing how both the novel and the film depict the psychological and social consequences of the violence that destroys Mano Majra's harmonious community.

**3.3 Displacement and Refugee Trauma**

The forced migration precipitated by the 1947 Partition constitutes one of the most extensive and traumatic displacements of populations in modern history, and both Khushwant Singh's novel *Train to Pakistan* and its cinematic adaptation labour to represent the profound psychological suffering that such dislocation entails. Displacement, as a concept, extends far beyond the mere physical relocation of individuals from one geographic site to another; it encompasses a fundamental rupture in the relationship between the self and the world, a form of existential disorientation that destabilises identity and memory. The theoretical framework for understanding this phenomenon distinguishes between personal trauma, which affects the individual psyche, and social trauma, which reverberates through entire communities. As Cathy Caruth articulates, trauma is not a simple act of forgetting but rather a condition of latency wherein the overwhelming event returns unbidden, "triggered by a similar event" [1, p. 125]. This distinction proves crucial for analysing the refugee experience in *Train to Pakistan*, for the novel demonstrates that the violence of Partition does not merely wound individuals in isolation but ruptures the very fabric of communal life, creating a shared condition of psychological suffering that persists long after the physical journey from one place to another has concluded.

The representation of displacement in both the novel and the film is structured around the concept of dismemberment, understood not merely as physical mutilation but as a violent severing of social bonds and geographic belonging. Matt Wanat theorises dismemberment as "de-membership" characterised by "social disintegration, geographic depopulation or dehumanization, and environmental devastation" [1, p. 149]. This formulation illuminates the multiple registers of loss that characters in Mano Majra experience when the order to evacuate Muslim residents is given. The novel meticulously documents the dissolution of relationships that had sustained the village for generations, relationships that transcended religious difference and constituted the very basis of communal identity. The departure from ancestral lands represents more than a change of address; it constitutes what Aanchal Malhotra describes as "a sudden up-rootedness, an unlearning and relearning of identity," a process accompanied by an "unavoidable disorientation" that the circumstances of 1947 would have pronounced with devastating force [2, p. 15]. This theoretical insight finds vivid textual expression in Singh's depiction of the farewell between Imam Baksh and Meet Singh, the Muslim and Sikh leaders of Mano Majra. When Imam Baksh declares, "What have we to do with Pakistan? We were born here. So were our ancestors. We have lived amongst you as brothers," the novel captures the profound absurdity of a political partition that contradicts lived experience [2, p. 80]. The subsequent image of Sikh and Muslim villagers "fall[ing] into each other's arms and weep[ing] like children" renders visible the trauma of dismemberment as a social wound that fractures a previously integrated community [2, p. 81].

Furthermore, the novel demonstrates that the trauma of displacement manifests not only in the moment of departure but also in the lingering psychological scars that continue to haunt those who survive. The character of Magistrate Hukum Chand serves as a primary vehicle for exploring the delayed and repetitive nature of traumatic experience. Upon witnessing the train filled with the corpses of Sikhs and Hindus massacred in Pakistan, Hukum Chand initially experiences a "cold numbness" that deadens his emotional responses [7, p. 5]. This numbing, however, gives way to a terrifying return of the repressed memory, as the magistrate finds himself unable to escape the nightmare vision of the dead: "He tried to shout but could only open his mouth… His voice stuck in his throat... Hukum Chand began to moan and then with a final effort broke out of the nightmare with an agonized shriek. He sat up with a look of terror in his eyes" [2, pp. 55-56]. This representation aligns precisely with Caruth's formulation of trauma as a condition in which the event returns "against their will" and in which the survivor's uncertainty is "not a simple amnesia" but rather the involuntary recurrence of the traumatic scene [1, pp. 124-125]. The novel thus suggests that displacement produces a form of temporal dislocation as well as geographic dislocation; the refugee or the witness to atrocity becomes trapped in a present that is perpetually invaded by the violent past.

The film adaptation, directed by Pamela Rooks, extends and visualises this representation of refugee trauma through its cinematographic choices, translating the psychological interiority of the novel into observable, visceral images. Where Singh's prose describes the emotional paralysis of characters, the film renders visible the bodily manifestations of trauma: the hollow stares, the hesitant movements, the physical collapse of individuals overwhelmed by grief. One of the most significant contributions of the cinematic adaptation lies in its ability to visualise the "hidden extensions of the fictionalized," as the directors "could brilliantly visualize the hidden lover (Juggut Singh) while climbing the ladder to cut off the robe of death for the purpose of rescuing the innocent civilians crowded in the train going to Pakistan" [6, p. 5]. This scene, which in the novel is rendered through Singh's restrained prose, becomes in the film a powerful visual testament to the possibility of human agency in the face of overwhelming collective trauma. The cinematic medium, through its capacity for what George Bluestone terms "making you see," can convey the "affective relationship between the creative artist and the receptive audience" in ways that complement but also differ from the literary imagination [6, p. 1]. The film thus provides a supplementary mode of testimony, one that makes the trauma of displacement legible through the body and the landscape rather than through internal monologue alone.

Moreover, the representation of refugee trauma in both the novel and the film is deeply gendered, revealing the specific vulnerabilities and forms of suffering that women experience in contexts of forced migration. The character of Nooran, the young Muslim woman in love with the Sikh dacoit Jugga, embodies the intersection of personal tragedy and political violence. When the order for the evacuation of Muslims is announced, Nooran finds herself caught between her love for Jugga and the communal logic that now defines her as the enemy. Her visit to Jugga's mother represents a desperate attempt to maintain a connection that the Partition has rendered impossible: "'All right, Beybey, I will go. Don't be angry with me. When Jugga comes back just tell him I came to say Sat Sri Akal.' The girl went down on her knees, clasped the old woman's legs, and began to sob. 'Beybey, I am going away and will never come back again. Don't be harsh to me just when I am leaving'" [9, p. 82]. The revelation that Nooran is pregnant with Jugga's child intensifies the tragedy, transforming the loss of love into the severance of future generations. This scene demonstrates that displacement is not merely a matter of leaving one's home but also of being torn from intimate relationships that constitute the very meaning of one's existence. The film adaptation captures the affective dimension of this loss through close-up shots that emphasise Nooran's isolation and the physicality of her grief, rendering visible what the novel conveys through dialogue and interiority.

In contrast to the personal trauma experienced by individual characters, the novel also represents displacement as a form of social trauma that fundamentally restructures the relationship between individuals and the state. The breakdown of administrative authority during the Partition creates a vacuum in which violence flourishes and those entrusted with protecting civilians become complicit in their suffering. Hukum Chand, despite his personal guilt and horror at the violence he witnesses, proves "largely ineffective" as a magistrate, and his "guilt-ridden conscience reflects the moral paralysis of authority during Partition" [15, p. 1]. The novel exposes the failure of the state to protect its citizens through its depiction of the decision to release the murderer Malli from prison, an act that directly contributes to violence against Muslims rather than preventing it. As the sub-inspector advises, "First, take Malli and his men to Mano Majra. Release them where the villagers can see them being released" [9, p. 71]. This cynical manipulation of communal sentiment demonstrates how the trauma of displacement is compounded by the betrayal of those institutions that should provide security. The film adaptation visualises this institutional collapse through its depiction of the chaos at the railway station, where soldiers and officials appear overwhelmed and directionless, unable to impose order on the escalating crisis.

The experience of displacement, as represented in both media, also produces a profound identity crisis among characters who must suddenly define themselves in communal terms that contradict their lived relationships. Before the Partition, the residents of Mano Majra understood themselves as members of a single village community; after the Partition, they are forced to become Sikhs, Muslims, or Hindus first and villagers second. This transformation is represented not as a natural evolution of identity but as a violent imposition that tears at the fabric of self-understanding. As one scholarly analysis notes, the novel explores "the changing perception of identity during the Violence," demonstrating how "Muslims hide their religious identity in predominantly Sikh areas, and vice versa," revealing "the fragility of identity and the fluidity with which individuals adapt to protect themselves" [11, p. 9]. The refugee, in this context, is not simply someone who has been forced to leave a physical location but someone who has been stripped of a stable sense of self. The novel's most powerful representation of this identity crisis occurs in the moment of farewell between Imam Baksh and Meet Singh, where both religious leaders weep openly, acknowledging that the identities imposed by the Partition are false to their lived experience of brotherhood. The film captures this moment with particular poignancy, using long takes and close-up shots to emphasise the emotional devastation of men who are being forced to become enemies against their will.

Ultimately, the representation of displacement and refugee trauma in *Train to Pakistan* and its film adaptation refuses the consolation of resolution or healing. There are "no winners—only victims" [15, p. 1]. The novel concludes with Jugga's sacrifice, an act that saves the train full of Muslims but at the cost of his own life, and the final image of "his body go[ing] limp, but the train [going] safely on to Pakistan" offers a fragile assertion of human dignity against the backdrop of systematic dehumanisation [15, p. 1]. This conclusion does not, however, undo the trauma that has been represented throughout the narrative; rather, it insists that individual acts of compassion can occur even within contexts of overwhelming violence. The film adaptation, constrained by its medium, must translate this literary conclusion into visual terms, and the directors succeed by focusing on the image of the train continuing its journey, a powerful symbol of the persistence of life even in the face of death. The representation of displacement in both media thus serves a testimonial function, preserving the memory of Partition not as an abstraction but as a lived experience of suffering, loss, and the struggle to maintain humanity in conditions designed to destroy it.

This analysis of displacement and refugee trauma reveals the intimate connection between political violence and psychological suffering, a connection that will be further explored in the following section's examination of how both the novel and the film represent the agency of subaltern characters.

### 3.4 Violence in the Film Adaptation

The cinematic adaptation of Khushwant Singh's *Train to Pakistan*, directed by Pamela Rooks in 1998, confronts the formidable challenge of translating the novel's visceral depictions of communal brutality into a visual language that both preserves the text's thematic gravity and exploits the unique affective capacities of the filmic medium. While the novel relies upon descriptive prose and the reader's imaginative engagement to convey the horror of Partition-era violence, the film must render such atrocities visible, immediate, and emotionally palpable through the strategic deployment of cinematography, mise-en-scène, sound, and editing. As Khalid Alqadhi observes, adaptation constitutes "a subjective interpretation of the director, inspired by a text, and then crafting his film differently," wherein the filmmaker may "insist on some passages of the story than others" or "expand a single move briefly described in a novel, to a three-shot sequence" [1, p. 45]. Rooks's adaptation exemplifies this interpretive process, particularly in its visual strategies for depicting violence, which oscillate between graphic realism and symbolic restraint, between the spectacle of mutilation and the haunting absence of the human form.

The most iconic and narratively pivotal representation of violence in the film adaptation is the arrival of the "ghost train," a locomotive carrying the mutilated corpses of massacred Sikhs and Hindus from Pakistan. In the novel, Singh describes this event through a gradual accumulation of sensory details: the smell of burning kerosene and wood, the "faint acrid smell of searing flesh," and the visual transformation of the horizon from "bluish-grey" to "orange" to "copper" and finally to "a luminous russet" [3, p. 2]. The film, however, must compress this sensory buildup into a concentrated visual and auditory experience. Rooks presents the ghost train's arrival as a slow, almost surreal sequence: the train emerges from the darkness, its headlamp cutting through the night, and as it passes, the camera lingers on the piled bodies visible through the open carriage doors. The soundtrack shifts from the ambient sounds of the village to an ominous silence punctuated by the rhythmic clanking of the train wheels, creating an atmosphere of dread that precedes the visual revelation of the corpses. This sequence functions not merely as a plot point but as a visual metaphor for the intrusion of national catastrophe into the pastoral isolation of Mano Majra. As one analysis notes, the ghost train "becomes a pivotal symbol of the communal violence that disrupts this idyllic life" [7, p. 1]. The film's visual treatment of this symbol—its slow, deliberate pacing and the use of low-key lighting to obscure and reveal the bodies—emphasizes the incomprehensibility of the violence, suggesting that the horror exceeds the capacity of the human gaze to fully apprehend it.

Furthermore, the film adaptation employs a distinctive visual strategy for depicting the aftermath of massacres through the recurring image of bloated human bodies floating down the river. In the novel, Singh provides a graphic description: "Some were without limbs, some had their bellies torn open, and many women's breasts were slashed. They floated down the sunlit river, bobbing up and down. Overhead hung the kites and vultures" [3, p. 2]. The film translates this passage into a long shot that captures the bodies drifting in the moonlight, their pale forms contrasting starkly with the dark water. This scene exemplifies what Alqadhi identifies as the "challenges of colorization" in the adaptation, wherein the director must "imitate the real color described in the text" while inevitably introducing "personal deviation" [5, p. 6]. Rooks chooses to film the river scene at night, using moonlight to create a silvery, almost ethereal quality that distances the viewer from the full graphic horror of the corpses. This aesthetic choice serves a dual purpose: it respects the novel's insistence on the reality of bodily mutilation while simultaneously acknowledging the limitations of the cinematic medium in representing trauma without descending into gratuitous spectacle. The vultures circling overhead, rendered as dark silhouettes against the moonlit sky, reinforce the sense of nature's indifference to human suffering, a theme that Singh develops through his restrained prose.

The film's portrayal of gendered violence constitutes another crucial dimension of its visual strategy. The novel consistently positions women as the primary victims of communal brutality, their bodies becoming "sites of revenge and humiliation" [10, p. 1]. Singh writes of women who "jumping into wells and burning themselves rather than falling into the hands of Muslims," and those who "were paraded naked in the streets, raped in public, and then murdered" [3, p. 2]. The film adaptation, constrained by the conventions of mainstream Indian cinema in the 1990s, cannot depict such explicit sexual violence directly. Instead, Rooks employs a strategy of visual suggestion and metonymy. The camera focuses on the faces of female characters—Nooran's silent terror, the grief-stricken expressions of refugee women—rather than on the acts of violation themselves. When the film shows the dead bodies of women floating in the river, the camera maintains a respectful distance, avoiding close-ups that might sensationalize the mutilation. As Alqadhi notes, "Pandey and Brooks seem careful in their film of *Train to Pakistan* to depict the same imaginative feeling on the faces of Nooran and Haseena and even the dead bodies of the murdered women floating in the river" [5, p. 6]. This approach aligns with the novel's broader critique of patriarchy, wherein women "look like subjects for being abducted or kidnapped, raped, killed and illegally exploited" [5, p. 6]. The film's visual restraint in depicting sexual violence paradoxically amplifies its horror by forcing the viewer to imagine what the camera refuses to show, thereby engaging the audience's own complicity in the construction of trauma.

In contrast to the symbolic restraint of the river scene, the film adaptation intensifies the physical and emotional immediacy of violence during its climactic sequence, wherein Jugga Singh attempts to prevent the derailment of the train carrying Muslim refugees. The novel builds toward this moment through extensive interior monologue and moral reflection, allowing the reader to understand Jugga's transformation from a reckless outlaw to a sacrificial hero. The film, however, must externalize this internal conflict through action and visual spectacle. As one comparative analysis observes, "The film places greater emphasis on the suspense and action surrounding the climax, where Jugga attempts to stop the train and save Nooran. This shift focuses on the physical and emotional intensity of the final moments" [2, p. 2]. Rooks employs rapid cross-cutting between Jugga running along the tracks, the train approaching, and the saboteurs preparing to detonate the explosives. The editing accelerates as the tension mounts, creating a visceral sense of urgency that the novel's more contemplative pace cannot replicate. The camera follows Jugga in handheld shots that convey his desperation and physical exhaustion, while the sound design layers the train's whistle, the shouts of the villagers, and the rhythmic pounding of Jugga's footsteps. When Jugga finally cuts the rope and is struck by the train, the film presents his death not as a heroic tableau but as a sudden, brutal interruption—a body going limp against the relentless momentum of the locomotive. This visual treatment underscores the novel's assertion that Jugga's sacrifice is "not heroic in a conventional sense; it is impulsive, emotional, and deeply human" [10, p. 1].

The film's depiction of mob violence and communal hatred further distinguishes its visual strategy from the novel's descriptive approach. Singh portrays the transformation of ordinary villagers into a bloodthirsty mob through psychological realism, showing how "collective fear and revenge override moral judgment" [10, p. 1]. The novel includes the chilling declaration of a villager: "I don't know who the Muslims on the train are; I do not care. It is enough for me to know that they are Muslims. They will not cross this river alive" [11, p. 3]. The film visualizes this transformation through the staging of crowd scenes, wherein the camera captures the gradual shift from individual hesitation to collective aggression. Rooks uses medium shots that isolate particular faces within the crowd, showing the moment when fear hardens into resolve, and then wider shots that dissolve these individuals into an undifferentiated mass. The lighting in these scenes becomes harsher, with shadows deepening as the moral darkness descends upon the village. The film also employs the visual motif of weapons—sickles, axes, and sticks—being passed from hand to hand, transforming agricultural tools into instruments of murder. This visual detail reinforces the novel's argument that the violence of Partition was not perpetrated by monsters but by ordinary people who "were not bad men. They were not murderers. But they were afraid" [10, p. 1]. The film's visual strategy thus emphasizes the banality of evil, showing how communal hatred can be normalized through collective action.

The use of music and sound in the film adaptation constitutes a significant visual-auroral strategy for representing the before and after of violence. The novel lacks a musical dimension, relying instead on descriptive passages to establish the peaceful rhythm of village life before the catastrophe. The film, however, introduces the song "Sanu Aa Milya Yaar Piyaariya," composed by Sai Bulleshah, which "plays a significant role in the film, setting the tone for the peaceful existence of the villagers before the chaos of Partition" [2, p. 2]. This song, with its evocative lyrics and imagery, "serves as a subtle narrative device to underscore the harmony that once existed in Mano Majra" [2, p. 2]. The visual accompaniment to this song—shots of villagers working in the fields, children playing, and the daily routines of domestic life—creates a pastoral idyll that the subsequent violence will shatter. When the ghost train arrives, the film's sound design shifts abruptly from the melodic strains of the song to the dissonant screech of metal and the silence of shock. This auditory rupture mirrors the visual rupture of the train's intrusion into the village landscape, reinforcing the novel's theme that Partition constituted a catastrophic break in the continuity of communal life.

The film's visual representation of the state's failure to prevent violence further distinguishes its adaptation from the novel. Singh portrays Magistrate Hukum Chand as a morally paralyzed figure whose "guilt-ridden conscience reflects the moral paralysis of authority during Partition" [10, p. 1]. The film visualizes this paralysis through the character's physical positioning within the frame. Hukum Chand is often shown isolated in his office, separated from the village by windows and doors that frame him as a passive observer rather than an active participant. The camera lingers on his face in close-up, capturing the micro-expressions of helplessness and guilt that the novel can only describe through interior monologue. When he witnesses the arrival of the ghost train, the film shows him standing at a distance, his figure dwarfed by the scale of the catastrophe. This visual strategy reinforces the novel's critique of administrative failure, suggesting that the state's representatives are not merely ineffective but fundamentally incapable of comprehending or responding to the violence unfolding before them. As one analysis notes, "Police officials and administrators appear confused, indifferent, or powerless" [10, p. 1], and the film's visual treatment of Hukum Chand embodies this critique through spatial and compositional choices.

In conclusion, the film adaptation of *Train to Pakistan* employs a sophisticated visual strategy for depicting violence that oscillates between graphic realism and symbolic restraint, between the spectacle of mutilation and the haunting absence of the human form. Rooks navigates the constraints of the cinematic medium—its need for condensation, its reliance on visual spectacle, and its sensitivity to the conventions of mainstream Indian cinema—while remaining faithful to the novel's core thematic concerns. The film's visual treatment of the ghost train, the river of corpses, gendered violence, mob psychology, and administrative failure demonstrates that adaptation is not merely a process of translation but of creative transformation, wherein the director's "subjective interpretation" [1, p. 45] generates new meanings and affective possibilities. The film succeeds in capturing "the emotional intensity of the story, emphasizing the personal sacrifices made against the backdrop of national tragedy" [2, p. 2], even as it necessarily condenses and streamlines the novel's more complex reflections on Partition. This analysis of violence in the film adaptation now leads to a consideration of how both the novel and the film construct the figure of the refugee and the experience of displacement, a theme that bridges the textual and cinematic representations of trauma.

### 3.5 Comparative Analysis of Violence in the Novel and Film

The representation of violence in Khushwant Singh’s novel *Train to Pakistan* and Pamela Rooks’s film adaptation reveals fundamental differences in how each medium constructs meaning from traumatic historical events. While both works share a central thematic commitment to depicting the human cost of the Partition, their respective formal properties—narrative prose versus cinematic visuality—generate distinct affective and interpretive experiences for the audience. The novel employs descriptive language and extended interiority to foster reflection on the moral and political dimensions of violence, whereas the film prioritizes visual immediacy and emotional intensity, condensing complex character motivations and subplots to heighten the dramatic impact of the climactic events. This comparative analysis demonstrates that the adaptation’s necessary condensation of narrative material results in a shift from the novel’s analytical engagement with systemic violence toward a more visceral, character-driven portrayal of personal sacrifice.

The novel’s treatment of violence relies heavily on graphic description that forces the reader to confront the physical reality of atrocity while simultaneously creating space for moral contemplation. Singh’s prose lingers on the details of mutilated bodies, as when the narrator describes corpses floating down the river: “Some were without limbs, some had their bellies torn open, and many women’s breasts were slashed” [5, p. 2]. Such passages do not merely report violence but compel the reader to imagine the scene, thereby generating a cognitive and emotional response that is mediated through language. The descriptive density of the novel allows Singh to embed violence within a broader social and political context, as evidenced by the portrayal of refugees who “had told of women jumping into wells and burning themselves rather than falling into the hands of Muslims” [5, p. 2]. This technique enables the novel to present violence as both a personal trauma and a collective historical phenomenon, inviting the reader to reflect on the systemic failures that enabled such brutality. Furthermore, the novel’s narrative structure, which alternates between the perspectives of multiple characters, permits an exploration of the psychological effects of violence on perpetrators, victims, and bystanders alike. The character of Hukum Chand, for instance, embodies the moral paralysis of administrative authority; he “understands the tragedy of Partition” yet “remains passive and ineffective,” suggesting that political ambition and institutional failure were complicit in the violence [13, p. 2]. Through such characterizations, the novel positions violence not as an aberration but as the logical outcome of communal hatred exacerbated by elite indifference.

In contrast, the film adaptation translates violence into visual and auditory registers that prioritize immediate emotional impact over reflective analysis. Rooks employs cinematic techniques such as close-ups, lighting, and sound to create a sense of urgency and horror that is qualitatively different from the novel’s descriptive prose. The arrival of the ghost train, a pivotal symbol in both works, is rendered in the film through stark imagery: the long shot of bloated human bodies floating down the river, viewed by the numbed inhabitants of Mano Majra in moonlight, generates a visceral response that bypasses the cognitive processing required by reading [12, p. 6]. The film’s use of the song “Sanu Aa Milya Yaar Piyaariya” further illustrates this shift, as the music “sets the tone for the peaceful existence of the villagers before the chaos of Partition,” functioning as a subtle narrative device that underscores the harmony soon to be destroyed [1, p. 2]. The novel lacks this musical element, relying instead on descriptive passages to evoke the same atmosphere. This difference highlights a fundamental constraint of the cinematic medium: where the novel can pause for introspection, the film must maintain momentum, focusing on “the physical and emotional intensity of the final moments” rather than the “larger political and social implications” that the novel explores at length [1, p. 2]. The film’s condensation of the timeline and streamlining of subplots—such as the minimization of Haseena’s character and the reduction of Iqbal’s political ideology—serve to concentrate attention on the central love story and the climactic sacrifice, thereby transforming violence from a systemic critique into a personal tragedy [2, p. 1].

The divergent treatment of character motivation further illuminates the differing representational strategies of the two media. The novel provides a rich exploration of the internal conflicts that drive characters toward or away from violence, whereas the film simplifies these motivations for narrative efficiency. In the novel, Jugga Singh is a complex figure whose criminal past and love for Nooran are intertwined with his evolving moral consciousness; his sacrifice emerges from a gradual process of self-realization that the reader witnesses through extended interior monologue and dialogue. The film, however, presents Jugga as a more straightforward hero, his actions driven by love and a desire for redemption without the same depth of psychological development [1, p. 1]. Similarly, Iqbal’s character in the novel is a “cynical intellectual who questions the efficacy of political solutions and grapples with the limitations of his own idealism,” whereas the film portrays him as “a more straightforward idealist, driven by a desire to bring about social change” [1, p. 1]. This simplification has significant implications for the representation of violence: the novel’s nuanced characterizations suggest that violence arises from a complex interplay of ideology, circumstance, and personal history, while the film’s streamlined portrayals risk reducing violence to a Manichaean conflict between good and evil. The novel’s depiction of mob mentality further complicates this picture, showing how “ordinary people” who “once lived peacefully become capable of cruelty and murder” when influenced by outside agitators [13, p. 2]. The film captures this transformation visually—through the angry faces of villagers preparing to attack the train—but cannot replicate the novel’s sustained analysis of how communal hatred “reduces people to religious identities and eliminates compassion” [13, p. 2].

The role of symbolism in each medium also diverges in ways that affect the representation of violence. In both the novel and the film, the train functions as a powerful symbol, initially representing British imperialism before transforming into an emblem of the carnage caused by Partition [2, p. 1]. However, the novel’s symbolic register is more expansive, allowing for extended meditation on the train’s multiple meanings across the narrative. The film, constrained by its runtime, must rely on visual shorthand: the ghost train’s arrival is a single, devastating image rather than a recurring motif that accumulates significance over time. This difference is particularly evident in the treatment of the climactic scene. In the novel, Jugga’s act of cutting the rope to prevent the train from being derailed is the culmination of a carefully constructed moral arc; the reader understands the weight of his sacrifice because the novel has devoted considerable space to exploring his relationship with Nooran and his growing disillusionment with communal violence. The film, while preserving the essential action, compresses this development, focusing instead on the suspense and action surrounding the climax [1, p. 2]. The result is that the film’s violence, while emotionally powerful, carries less analytical weight; it signifies personal heroism rather than the systemic failure that the novel critiques.

The comparative analysis reveals that the adaptation’s fidelity to the novel’s central themes coexists with significant formal and interpretive shifts. The film succeeds in capturing “the emotional intensity of the story, emphasizing the personal sacrifices made against the backdrop of national tragedy,” but the “deeper, more complex reflections on Partition present in Singh’s novel are somewhat diminished” [1, p. 2]. This diminution is not merely a loss but a transformation inherent in the process of adaptation: the cinematic medium privileges visual impact and narrative economy, which necessarily alters the audience’s relationship to violence. Where the novel invites the reader to pause, reflect, and analyze, the film compels the viewer to feel, react, and empathize. Both approaches have validity, but they produce different understandings of the Partition’s violence. The novel’s analytical distance allows for a critique of the political and social structures that enabled atrocity, while the film’s emotional immediacy foregrounds the human cost of those structures. This distinction is crucial for understanding how each medium contributes to the cultural memory of the Partition.

This comparative analysis of violence in the novel and film adaptation sets the stage for a broader consideration of how literary and cinematic representations of historical trauma shape collective memory. The next section will examine the reception of both works, exploring how audiences and critics have interpreted their respective portrayals of violence and what this reveals about the evolving discourse surrounding the Partition in Indian cultural history.
