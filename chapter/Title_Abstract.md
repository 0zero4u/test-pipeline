# Abstract

This dissertation undertakes a comparative analysis of Khushwant Singh's novel *Train to Pakistan* (1956) and its cinematic adaptation directed by Iqbal Sehgal (1998), examining how partition violence and humanism are reconfigured across textual and visual registers. Employing a methodology grounded in comparative textual and cinematic analysis, the study explores the narrative, structural, and ideological transformations that occur when literary humanism migrates into filmic representation. The analysis reveals three principal findings. First, Singh's novel strategically subverts dominant nationalist narratives by deploying affective politics that foreground individual moral choice against collective sectarian violence. Second, the film adaptation fundamentally transforms these literary strategies through visual representation, employing cinematic techniques of framing, lighting, and mise-en-scene to reimagine the relationship between historical trauma and individual agency. Third, both texts operate within a persistent tension between collective trauma and individual redemption, negotiating the ethical imperatives of witnessing while preserving space for humanistic hope. By demonstrating how both novel and film resist reductive readings of partition history, this dissertation contributes to scholarly conversations by highlighting the necessity for critical approaches that move beyond celebratory accounts of literary humanism, instead accounting for the ideological work performed by aesthetic choices in representing partition violence.
