# Chapter III

# Partition and Violence in *Train to Pakistan*

The Partition of British India in August 1947 was one of the largest population movements in modern history. Between fourteen and eighteen million people lost their homes. The violence that followed killed anywhere from two hundred thousand to two million people. Different historians give different figures (Butalia 7). Many books and films have been made about this disaster. Among them, Khushwant Singh's 1956 novel *Train to Pakistan* stands out. The book does more than tell the story of Partition. It asks hard questions about guilt, silence, and whether people can resist when violence spreads through a community. The 1998 film, directed by Pamela Rooks, takes these same questions and puts them on screen. It uses camera work, set design, and acting to make its own moral argument about the violence of 1947. This chapter looks at how both the novel and the film portray partition violence. It shows how each medium shapes, beautifies, and questions the trauma of 1947. Through close reading and comparison, the chapter argues that Singh's novel gets its power from restraint and irony. The film, by contrast, uses strong images and emotional impact to make the same violence real for an audience more used to watching films than reading books.

## 3.1 Historical Violence in the Novel: The Representation of Partition Riots and Brutality

Khushwant Singh's *Train to Pakistan* is set in a fictional village called Mano Majra. This Punjabi village sits on the border between India and Pakistan. The story begins when Partition arrives. The novel opens with the local magistrate, Ikgar Singh, playing chess. This opening sets the tone. The book is about the tension between order and chaos. It is about law and violence. It is about habit and crisis. Singh does not start with violence. He starts with stillness. The violence comes later. It breaks into the narrative like a crack in a wall. This approach mirrors what actually happened. Most Punjab villages in August 1947 had weeks or months of normal life before the killing started. The social fabric frayed slowly. It did not tear all at once.

The historical violence in the novel cannot be separated from the political context of 1947. The story takes place during the British withdrawal. It involves the Radcliffe Award and how territory was divided. It shows the communal tension that politicians stirred up. Singh does not give a long political history of Partition. Instead, he weaves political context into daily life in Mano Majra. When the Jwandwal train brings refugees to the village, the village gets pulled into events far larger than itself. The train becomes a symbol of the violence. It carries the dead and the dying. It stops the farming routines of the village. It turns a quiet place into a stage for a national disaster.

Singh's portrayal of the brutalities is notable for what it does not show. The novel does not give graphic descriptions of massacre, rape, or physical violence. Some Partition memoirs do this. Singh does not. He conveys the reality of violence through its effects on people and the community. He also conveys it through what characters say and do not say. The character of Jugga, the village strongman, represents the novel's engagement with violence. He is both physical and moral. Jugga starts as a local tough who steals another man's wife. Later, he faces the chance to take part in killing refugees. His role shifts. This change is not shown as heroic or redemptive. Singh treats it with the same ironic detachment he uses for all his characters. He refuses to make Jugga's choice sentimental. He also refuses to make the community's failure to resist sentimental.

The social and political sides of violence in the novel are clearest in the character of Lala Raghunath. He is a Sikh politician who arrives at the Mano Majra station. He gives a speech to the villagers. Lala Raghunath stands for the political class that sparked violence while staying safe from its effects. His speech is carefully crafted to appeal to communal identity. It frames refugees as threats rather than as fellow victims. Singh's treatment of Lala Raghunath is sharp and satirical. The politician is self-interested. He is opportunistic. He uses communal hatred for his own political gain. This satirical portrait makes a larger point. Partition violence was not a sudden explosion of ancient hatred. It was manufactured. Political actors deliberately created conflict because they profited from it.

The violence in the novel also reveals the failure of institutional authority. The magistrate Ikgar Singh stands for the British colonial state. He is shown as a man whose importance is fading. His power rested on the stability of colonial rule. That stability collapsed as the violence grew. He could not stop the massacre that threatened the station. This portrayal reflects what actually happened in August 1947. British authority fell apart just as the violence it had long controlled was released without any restraint. Singh's magistrate is not a villain. He is a relic. His skills mean nothing against an event that has made those skills irrelevant. The political side of the violence is also a side of historical change. The novel shows the moment when the old order dies and the new order has not yet been born.

In terms of literary style, Singh uses restraint as his main tool for representing violence. This restraint comments on the nature of Partition violence. The horror lies not in what can be described but in what is so common that it needs no extended explanation. The narrator notes that the village has grown used to hearing about massacres in nearby towns. This accommodation to violence is one of the novel's most disturbing elements. The casualness with which characters discuss the killing reflects what historian Ritu Menon called the normalization of Partition brutality. Extraordinary violence became part of daily life (Menon and Bhasin 14). Singh's technique refuses to sensationalize. It mirrors the normalization while also criticizing it.

## 3.2 Communal Breakdown in Mano Majra: The Collapse of Communal Harmony

The village of Mano Majra in Singh's novel is a small version of Punjab society at the moment of Partition. Before the train and refugees arrive, the village is shown as a place where different communities live together. The Sikh family, the Muslim family, and the Hindu household share the same space. Their daily lives involve farming, religious observance, and social duty. Singh does not paint an ideal picture of pre-Partition harmony. He is too clear-eyed for that. He does suggest that the village had found a working, if imperfect, way to coexist. Communal difference existed. It was not yet politically weaponized.

The breakdown of this communal order is one of the novel's main concerns. Singh traces the stages of this breakdown with care. The first stage is rumor and worry. Villagers hear reports of violence in distant towns. They start to fear it might reach them. This stage features uncertainty and the start of withdrawal. Muslim villagers begin to stay closer to home. Casual interactions become more careful. The second stage is the arrival of outside actors. Lala Raghunath and other politicians and agitators come to the village. They come specifically to stir up communal feeling. Singh shows these actors as foreign to the village's social fabric. They are not from Mano Majra. Their interest in the village is practical, not communal. This portrayal matters historically. It matches scholarship that shows how outside agitators triggered local violence (Pandey 112).

The relationships among the three main religious communities in the village are complicated. They change as the story moves forward. The Sikh characters, especially Ikgar Singh and the refugee families, are shown as potential targets rather than perpetrators. The novel does not simplify the picture. Jugga, the Sikh strongman, is positioned as a possible perpetrator. His involvement stays uncertain until the novel's climax. The Muslim villagers face an increasingly impossible situation. As Muslims, they might be expected to benefit from Partition and Pakistan. But the violence threatens them as much as it threatens Sikhs and Hindus. Singh complicates the picture. He shows Muslim villagers who do not want to leave. They have deep roots in the village. They are caught between religious identity and the bonds of local community.

The breakdown of communal harmony is also a breakdown of language and behavior. Characters who used to talk easily now speak with new formality. They speak with new caution and new suspicion. The novel pays close attention to these shifts in social interaction. It suggests that violence is not only physical. It is also the destruction of the ordinary ways of living that make coexistence possible. Singh's portrayal of communal breakdown is historically specific and anthropologically rich. It shows the particular events of Partition. It also illuminates the general mechanisms by which communities fall apart under political violence.

The character who most fully represents the complexity of communal identity is Hukum Chand. He is the local policeman tasked with keeping order. Hukum Chand is a Hindu. His position puts him at the crossroads of institutional power and communal feeling. His gradual surrender to the mob is one of the novel's most troubling portrayals. It suggests that institutional power offers no reliable protection against the pull of communal loyalty. Hukum Chand is not a monster. He is an ordinary man who cannot resist a collective force that is both terrifying and, in the short term, satisfying. Singh's treatment of Hukum Chand reflects a broader concern in Partition literature. The question is about ordinary complicity. Who is responsible for the violence? What is moral responsibility in a time of mass atrocity?

The film adaptation, directed by Pamela Rooks, approaches communal breakdown with different tools. Where Singh traces the social mechanisms of dissolution through language and narrative, the film uses visual and spatial contrast. The film's early scenes establish Mano Majra as a coherent and visually ordered space. Tension grows through changes in this visual order. The lighting becomes harsher. Camera movements become more anxious. The spatial relationships among characters carry more unspoken threat. The director uses close-up shots to capture the micro-expressions of fear and suspicion on village faces. These close-ups serve a purpose similar to Singh's narrative restraint. They suggest that the most significant violence is not the physical kind still to come. It is the invisible violence of fear and distrust already corroding the community.

## 3.3 Displacement and Refugee Trauma: Forced Migration and Psychological Suffering

The refugee crisis of Partition is at the heart of *Train to Pakistan*. It drives the story as both a historical event and a narrative engine. The train that gives the novel its title arrives in Mano Majra carrying refugees who fled violence in western Punjab. Their arrival transforms the village. It turns from a place of ordinary rural life into a stage for historical crisis. Singh's portrayal of the refugees has a double perspective. The refugees are both sources of narrative suspense. Will they be killed or saved? They are also symbols of the massive human displacement that Partition caused. The refugees do not become individuals in the novel. They function as a group. They embody the countless anonymous masses displaced in August 1947.

This collective portrayal is a deliberate literary choice. Singh does not give the refugees names. He does not give them detailed backgrounds or individual voices. Saadat Hasan Manto did this in his short stories about Partition. Singh does not. The refugees exist as a condition. They are a pressure on the village and its people. This technique highlights the scale and impersonality of the displacement. It also highlights the moral question the village faces. The refugees are not individuals with personal claims on the villagers. They are also not strangers. They are co-religionists. They are fellow Punjabis. Their language and customs are familiar, even if their faces are not. The moral problem of the refugees is not reducible to personal ethics. It is a question about the nature of community and obligation when community itself is in crisis.

The psychological suffering of displacement, as shown in the novel, comes through physical description and behavioral observation. It does not come through direct psychological narration. Singh describes the refugees as exhausted, hungry, and traumatized. He does not give extended interior monologues or psychological analysis of their suffering. Instead, he shows the effects through the responses of the villagers. There is the decision to withhold food. There is the debate about sheltering or expelling the refugees. There is the growing worry about what the refugees' presence means for the village. This technique shifts the focus from the refugees to the community that must respond to them. It raises the question the novel takes as its central moral concern. What does the presence of suffering do to those who witness it?

The film adaptation handles refugee trauma through different cinematic strategies. The director's most powerful tool is the physical representation of the refugees' condition. Their ragged clothing. Their hollow eyes. Their closeness to one another in the crowded carriage. The visual representation of refugee bodies makes an argument about the nature of Partition violence. It insists that the trauma of displacement is a bodily trauma. It is not merely psychological or political. The film also uses sound to convey the refugees' suffering. The wailing of women. The crying of children. The ominous silence of men rendered speechless by their experience. These sounds create a sensory environment of suffering. They complement and intensify the visual representation of displacement.

The loss experienced by the refugees, in both novel and film, is not merely material. Both texts suggest that the refugees lost more than homes, property, and family. They lost the coherent narrative of their own lives. Displacement for Partition refugees was not only a geographical event. It was also a rupture in personal and collective identity. The refugees on the train had to abandon the story they understood about themselves. They arrive in Mano Majra as people without a history. They depend on the hospitality of strangers who are themselves increasingly uncertain about what comes next. This portrayal of displacement as a loss of narrative coherence is one of the most powerful aspects of both works. It matches the scholarly understanding that refugee trauma is fundamentally a disruption of biographical continuity (Malkki 23).

Fear is the dominant emotional note for both refugees and villagers in the novel's climactic scenes. Villagers fear that the refugees will bring violence to their door. Refugees fear they will be killed before reaching safety. This mutual fear is one of the novel's most psychologically accurate portrayals. It captures how Partition violence fed on itself. Each act of violence created fear. Each instance of fear generated more violence. Singh portrays this spiral with precision. He shows how it works at the level of individual psychology and at the level of collective behavior. The novel's strongest scenes are those in which characters are trapped by their fear. They cannot act. They cannot communicate. They increasingly cannot tell genuine threat from paranoid projection.

The displacement narrative in the novel also involves questions of gender and vulnerability. The refugees include women and children at particular risk of sexual violence. The novel's treatment of this vulnerability is notable for its restraint and moral seriousness. Singh does not describe sexual violence directly. He conveys the threat through the behavior of male villagers. They argue about what should be done with the women. He conveys it through the silence of the women themselves. They become increasingly Othered in the narrative. This portrayal, while not explicit, reflects the historical reality. Women's bodies became sites of communal violence during Partition. The question of what would happen to women was one of the most urgent and most troubling concerns raised by the displacement (Menon and Bhasin 89).

## 3.4 Violence in the Film Adaptation: Visual Portrayal of Brutality and Communal Tension

Pamela Rooks's 1998 film adaptation of *Train to Pakistan* came more than four decades after Partition. This time gap shapes the film's relationship to historical violence in important ways. Singh's novel was written in the immediate aftermath of Partition. The memory of the violence was still raw and contested. The film was made at a historical distance. That distance allows for a more self-conscious engagement with the aesthetics of violence. The film is not a neutral translation of the novel into visual terms. It is an interpretation. It reflects the cultural conditions of late-1990s India. These include the revival of Partition as a subject of public memory. They include the rise of a more commercially oriented film industry. They include the changing relationship between Indian cinema and questions of historical trauma.

The visual portrayal of violence in the film features a tension between restraint and spectacle. The film does not avoid depicting the consequences of violence. There are scenes showing bodies, destruction, and the physical evidence of massacre. However, these scenes are carefully composed. The cinematography treats the violence with a visual dignity that resists sensationalism. That sensationalism often marks popular cinema's treatment of trauma. The director uses long takes and wide shots to show the aftermath of violence. They force the viewer to contemplate the scale of destruction. They do not offer quick cuts that might allow the viewer to look away. This visual strategy matches the novel's own technique of restraint. It suggests that the film, like the novel, is more interested in the moral implications of violence than in the sensory experience of it.

Communal tension is established in the film through a mix of visual and behavioral codes. The film's treatment of Sikh and Muslim communities uses visual markers, costumes, and spatial division. These establish communal difference before the violence begins. This pre-violent phase of the film is crucial. It sets the baseline of communal life from which the subsequent breakdown must be measured. The film uses the physical environment of the village. The gurdwara, the mosque, and the fields around the village create a visual map of communal difference. This map is integrated into daily village life. It also highlights the boundaries between communities.

The film's most powerful depiction of communal tension comes in the sequences before the expected massacre at the railway station. The camera lingers on the faces of villagers preparing for what they expect to happen. Close-up shots capture the range of emotional responses to the impending violence. Anticipation. Fear. Uncertainty. Resolve. The particular blankness that marks a face when someone has decided to suspend moral judgment in favor of communal loyalty. These close-ups are among the film's most effective tools for depicting the psychology of complicity. They show that the violence is not committed by anonymous others. It is committed by recognizable individuals. The viewer has learned to read their faces over the course of the story.

The cinematic representation of trauma in the film works through several registers. The first is the physical representation of wounded and dead bodies. The camera photographs them with documentary authenticity. It insists on the materiality of the violence. The second is the soundscape. It combines diegetic sounds, screaming and weeping and the clatter of the train, with non-diegetic music. Together they create an emotional environment that is both horrific and melancholy. The third is the portrayal of character behavior under the pressure of violence. The film depicts this through performances that emphasize restraint, internal conflict, and the effort to maintain composure when events threaten to overwhelm it.

The film's director is especially attentive to the portrayal of female characters in the context of violence. The refugee woman appears in the novel as a collective symbol of vulnerability. She is individualized in the film to a greater extent. This individualization allows the film to explore the gendered dimensions of Partition violence with more specificity. Scenes showing refugee women in the train carriage, separated from male family members and exposed to the gaze of hostile villagers, create a visual argument. The argument concerns the relationship between violence, gender, and power. It complements and extends the novel's more oblique treatment of the same subject.

The adaptation also introduces visual motifs not found in the novel. These include extended sequences showing the refugees' journey and the landscape of Punjab as it is transformed by violence. These sequences use landscape cinematography to convey the scope of the displacement. Fields, rivers, and villages stretch to the horizon. All are marked by the passage of refugee columns. This use of landscape functions as a visual argument about the historical scale of Partition. It contrasts the intimate human drama of the village with the vast geographical dimension of the catastrophe.

## 3.5 Comparative Analysis: Violence in the Novel and the Film

The relationship between Singh's novel and Rooks's film adaptation is one of productive tension rather than simple loyalty. The film takes the novel's story as its foundation. It transforms that story through the different possibilities of the cinematic medium. The most significant difference lies in their respective approaches to the representation of violence. The novel achieves its effects through linguistic indirection, ironic restraint, and the strategic withholding of graphic detail. The film relies on visual presence, cinematic composition, and the emotional weight of the actor's face and body. These are not merely technical differences. They reflect different ways of knowing and different ethical stances toward the historical event of Partition and its representation.

One of the most significant points of comparison is their treatment of moral agency. In the novel, the question of who bears responsibility for the violence is addressed through characterization and narrative comment. Singh presents multiple characters who could act to prevent the violence. He shows how each of them fails to act, for different reasons and in different ways. This multivalent portrayal of moral failure is one of the novel's most sophisticated achievements. It resists simplifying responsibility into a single story of villainy or heroism. The film, by contrast, emphasizes moral agency through visual and performative choices. The director frames key scenes to highlight the individual choices faced by characters. The performances of the lead actors convey the internal conflict of characters deciding whether to take part in or resist the violence. The film offers a more dramatized version of the novel's moral inquiry. It uses the tools of cinema to make the ethical stakes more immediately clear to the viewer.

The representation of communal identity differs in important ways between the two works. The novel, as a literary work, has access to the inner lives of its characters in a way the film does not. Singh uses this access to show how communal identity is experienced from the inside. It is a complex and often contradictory set of affiliations and loyalties. The film, working through external signs and visual markers, tends to represent communal identity in more categorical terms. Sikh characters are visually distinguished from Muslim characters. The narrative arc of the film moves from communal difference to either communal violence or communal transcendence. This is not a failing of the film. It is a function of the medium's reliance on visible signs to convey identity. However, it does mean that the film's representation of communal complexity is less nuanced than the novel's. The film is more likely to suggest that communal identity is a stable and visible category. The novel shows it as fluid and internally contested.

The treatment of the refugees, central to both works, also differs in instructive ways. The novel's collective representation of the refugees emphasizes their anonymity and their status as victims of historical forces beyond their control. The refugees are not individualized because their individuality is not the point. The point is that they represent the experience of mass displacement. That experience is by definition an experience of the dissolution of individual identity. The film is forced to individualize the refugees to some extent. Cinema requires visible faces and bodies. The film uses this forced individualization strategically. It allows the camera to linger on particular refugee faces. This restores to these individuals a dignity and specificity that the novel's collective approach tends to submerge. This is one area in which the film arguably surpasses its source material. It uses the medium's tendency toward visual particularity to complicate and enrich the novel's representation of displacement.

In terms of narrative structure, the novel's ironic mode allows it to maintain a distance from the violence it depicts. This distance is the source of much of the novel's moral authority. Singh's narrator does not tell the reader what to feel. The ironic texture of the narrative invites the reader to make their own ethical assessments. The film, operating through the more immediate registers of image and sound, has less room for ironic distance. The visual presence of suffering creates an emotional demand that the novel can avoid. The director is aware of this and tries to manage it through the visual strategies of restraint described above. But the fundamental difference between the media means that the film's relationship to the viewer's emotions is always more direct and more insistent than the novel's relationship to its reader.

The ending of the novel and the ending of the film represent a crucial point of divergence. Singh's novel ends with ambiguity that has sparked considerable critical debate. The train departs, carrying the refugees to safety. The villagers are left to contemplate what happened and what they failed to do. The novel does not resolve the moral questions it raises. This openness is central to its power as a work of literature about historical trauma. The film adapts this ambiguous ending but adds visual and musical elements. These tip the resolution slightly in the direction of hope. They suggest that the survival of the refugees is a form of resistance that matters in itself. This adaptation is interpretively significant. It reveals the film's interest in commemorating the survival of the refugees as an event in itself. It does not treat survival as merely the occasion for further moral reflection.

Both the novel and the film are engaged in the project of bearing witness to the violence of Partition. Both do so with seriousness and moral urgency. They stand apart from more sensationalized or commercially motivated accounts of the same events. The differences between the two works show that the representation of historical trauma is not a fixed act. It is a process that varies according to the medium, the historical moment, and the cultural context of production. Singh's novel, written in the mid-1950s, speaks in the voice of a writer still close enough to the events to feel their raw urgency. He is distant enough to maintain a critical perspective. Rooks's film, made in the late 1990s, speaks from a position of historical reflection. It allows for a more visually elaborated and emotionally self-conscious engagement with the same material. Together, the two works offer a richer and more complex account of Partition violence than either could achieve alone. Their comparison illuminates not only the violence of 1947 but also the challenges and possibilities of representing that violence in different artistic forms.
