# Chapter I — Introduction

# 1.1 Historical Background: The Partition of India

The Partition of British India in August 1947 constitutes one of the most transformative and traumatic events in modern South Asian history. This division of the subcontinent into two sovereign nations—India and Pakistan—was not merely a political reconfiguration of territorial boundaries but a cataclysmic rupture that reshaped the social fabric, communal relations, and individual identities of millions [1, p. 9]. Understanding the complex historical circumstances surrounding this event is essential for analyzing how literary texts such as Khushwant Singh’s *Train to Pakistan* engage with the themes of violence, humanism, and communal harmony that form the central concerns of this dissertation.

The political origins of the Partition lie in the protracted struggle for independence from British colonial rule and the intensifying tensions between the Indian National Congress and the Muslim League. British colonial policy had long employed a strategy of "divide and rule," which deliberately exacerbated religious divisions to consolidate imperial control [6, p. 1]. The introduction of separate electorates for Hindus and Muslims fortified communal identities and institutionalized religious division within the political structure of colonial India [5, p. 8]. Over time, the demand for a separate Muslim nation, articulated most forcefully by the Muslim League under Muhammad Ali Jinnah, gained momentum, culminating in the eventual creation of Pakistan in August 1947 [6, p. 1]. This political trajectory demonstrates how communal divisions were not organic to Indian society but were, to a significant extent, manufactured and amplified by colonial governance and elite political competition.

The execution of the Partition was characterized by profound inadequacy in planning and implementation, reflecting the political leaders' lack of experience and maturity in managing such an unprecedented undertaking [1, p. 9]. The decision to divide British India was driven by superficial political calculations based on religion, with insufficient thought given to the human consequences of the division [1, p. 9]. As scholars have observed, the unplanned manner in which the leaders proceeded to partition the country transformed what could have been a celebration of independence into a catastrophe accompanied by horrendous tragic events [7, p. 2]. The political leadership proved incapable of controlling the situation once violence erupted, and the government failed to protect innocent citizens from the ensuing chaos [6, p. 1]. This administrative failure compounded the suffering of ordinary people who were caught unprepared for the violence that engulfed their communities.

The immediate aftermath of the Partition unleashed violence of an unprecedented scale and brutality. Communal riots broke out across Punjab, Bengal, Delhi, and several other regions, with Punjab becoming the epicenter of violence due to its mixed population of Hindus, Muslims, and Sikhs [6, p. 1]. Trains carrying refugees were attacked, villages were destroyed, and thousands of innocent people were killed in what scholars have described as an undeclared civil war [4, p. 1]. The violence encompassed not only mass murder but also systematic rape, kidnapping, and butchering of civilians [1, p. 9]. Approximately seventeen million people were displaced from their homes, forced to relocate across newly drawn boundaries in search of safety [7, p. 2]. The sheer scale of human suffering—the murder, rape, displacement, dismemberment, and trauma endured by common people—constitutes what one scholar terms the "unmentioned reality" of Partition, a reality that official histories of both India and Pakistan have systematically overlooked and left out [1, p. 9].

The violence of Partition had particularly devastating consequences for women, who experienced the gendered dimensions of communal conflict in uniquely brutal ways. Scholars such as Veena Das, Urvashi Butalia, Ritu Menon, and Kamla Bhasin have examined the relationship between communalism and nationality by focusing on the experiences of women and children during Partition [4, p. 2]. Their work reveals how women's bodies became sites of communal violence and how the trauma of sexual violence and abduction shaped women's identities long after the immediate violence subsided [3, p. 3]. Furthermore, oral histories and personal narratives have documented how women faced immense challenges in reconstructing their identities within new social and cultural contexts following the Partition [3, p. 3]. These gendered dimensions of violence underscore the complexity of the Partition's impact on individual lives and collective identities.

The psychological trauma inflicted by the Partition extended far beyond the physical violence and displacement. As Cathy Caruth's theoretical framework on trauma suggests, traumatic events are characterized by overwhelming experiences that produce delayed and uncontrolled repetitive responses, including hallucinations and intrusive phenomena [10, p. 5]. The Partition generated precisely such traumatic responses in survivors, who experienced deep shock and terrorizing violence that reoccurred in their minds with great intensity [10, p. 5]. The unprecedented nature of the violence—the sight of dead bodies, the destruction of homes, the forced separation from loved ones—created a collective trauma that has persisted across generations [9, p. 1]. This psychological dimension of the Partition is particularly significant for understanding the literary responses to this historical event, as writers sought to capture not only the physical destruction but also the emotional and psychological devastation experienced by ordinary people.

Importantly, the communal violence that characterized the Partition did not arise naturally from pre-existing animosities between religious communities. Before the Partition, Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, and Christians had lived together in relative harmony for centuries, sharing daily life, celebrating festivals together, and depending on one another economically and socially [6, p. 1]. The decision to partition the country was not the intent of common people but rather the superficial decision of hollow and selfish political leaders [1, p. 9]. Ordinary people were not ready for the situation of Partition or for the possible violence that accompanied it [1, p. 9]. The harmonious coexistence that had characterized many communities, particularly in rural areas, demonstrates that religious diversity did not inherently produce conflict. Rather, the violence was orchestrated by political actors who manipulated religious identities for their own interests, while evil persons took advantage of the situation to cause further violence [1, p. 9]. This distinction between the political elite who engineered division and the ordinary people who suffered its consequences is crucial for understanding the moral dimensions of the Partition.

Two nations emerged from this violent rupture: India, predominantly Hindu, and Pakistan, predominantly Muslim [2, p. 1]. Both countries began their independence with ruined economies and without a long-term, experienced system of state governance [2, p. 1]. The legacy of the Partition continued to shape national identities and international relations in the subcontinent for decades to come. The fanatic leaders of both communities had played a prominent role in stoking the fire of communalism, and the consequences of their actions reverberated through the social and political life of both nations [2, p. 1]. The Partition thus represents not only a historical event but also a cautionary tale about the dangers of political decisions made without adequate consideration of their human costs.

The literary response to the Partition, particularly in the genre of the novel, provides a unique perspective on this historical event that official histories often fail to capture. While historical accounts may document the dates, numbers, and political decisions of the Partition, fictional narratives portray both the human and political problems, acting as critiques of political history and providing insights into the reality of Partition politics [7, p. 2]. Khushwant Singh's *Train to Pakistan* stands as one of the most significant literary works to emerge from this historical context, offering a nuanced portrayal of characters navigating the complexities of identity and belonging amidst the violence and chaos of the Partition [3, p. 3]. Singh himself witnessed the horrors of Partition, and his personal experiences influenced the writing of the novel, which reflects the emotional and psychological trauma experienced by ordinary people during this period [6, p. 1].

The transition from the broader historical context of the Partition to the specific fictional world of *Train to Pakistan* requires an examination of how the novel represents and interprets this history. As the following section will demonstrate, Singh's novel engages with the political and social realities of the Partition by focusing on a single village, Mano Majra, whose peaceful communal harmony is shattered by the violence of Partition. This microcosmic approach allows the novel to explore how ordinary people experience and respond to extraordinary historical events, providing a human-scale perspective on a catastrophe that official histories often reduce to abstract numbers and political decisions. The subsequent analysis will examine how Singh's narrative constructs the pre-Partition harmony of Mano Majra, the intrusion of violence, and the moral choices faced by characters caught between their communal identities and their common humanity.

**1.2 About Khushwant Singh**

The literary career of Khushwant Singh constitutes a significant corpus within post-colonial Indian English literature, distinguished by its engagement with the tumultuous historical events that shaped the subcontinent in the mid-twentieth century. As a novelist, historian, journalist, and social commentator, Singh occupies a unique position in the Indian literary landscape, one that bridges the gap between journalistic reportage and imaginative fiction. His contribution to Partition literature, particularly through the novel *Train to Pakistan*, has established him as a foundational voice in the representation of the violence and human tragedy that accompanied the division of India in 1947. Understanding Singh's biographical background, his literary development, and his philosophical orientation is essential for comprehending the particular humanistic vision that animates his Partition narrative.

Khushwant Singh's personal history and professional trajectory equipped him with a distinctive perspective on the events of Partition. Born in 1915 in Hadali, Punjab (now in Pakistan), Singh belonged to a prominent Sikh family with deep roots in the region that would later be bisected by the new international border [1, p. 1]. His education at Government College, Lahore, and subsequently at King's College, London, and the Inner Temple exposed him to both Eastern and Western intellectual traditions, a dual heritage that would inform his cosmopolitan worldview [3, p. 1]. Before turning to full-time writing, Singh pursued a career in law and then in diplomacy, serving as a press attaché in London and Ottawa, and later as a journalist and editor for major Indian publications [5, p. 2]. This professional background provided him with a breadth of experience that few of his literary contemporaries possessed, enabling him to approach the subject of Partition not merely as a novelist but as an observer who had witnessed the political machinations and social upheavals of the period firsthand. The combination of his legal training, his diplomatic service, and his journalistic instincts created a writer uniquely positioned to document the complexities of India's independence and its attendant violence.

Singh's literary output spans multiple genres, but his reputation as a novelist rests primarily on a small number of works that have achieved canonical status in Indian English literature. Among these, *Train to Pakistan* (originally titled *Mano Majra*) stands as his most celebrated achievement, winning the Grove Press India Fiction Prize in 1956 and earning international acclaim [1, p. 1; 9, p. 1]. The novel emerged from Singh's direct experience of the Partition violence; as he later recounted, the events of 1947 left an indelible impression on his consciousness, compelling him to bear witness through fiction to the horrors he had observed [5, p. 2]. The work's initial title, *Mano Majra*, named after the fictional village that serves as the novel's setting, was changed to *Train to Pakistan* for its American edition, a modification that shifted the focus from the specific community to the larger movement of populations across the newly drawn border [5, p. 2]. This change in title reflects the novel's dual concern: the intimate life of a single village and the cataclysmic historical forces that disrupt it. Singh's other major novels, including *Delhi: A Novel* and *The Company of Women*, further explore themes of Indian history, identity, and social change, but none has achieved the critical and popular resonance of his Partition narrative [1, p. 1].

The humanistic philosophy that pervades Singh's writing constitutes the central organizing principle of his literary vision. Critics have consistently identified Singh as a humanist who approaches life "from the pit of his heart than the cerebellum," prioritizing emotional engagement and moral conviction over intellectual abstraction [7, p. 2]. This humanistic orientation manifests in his fiction through a persistent concern with ordinary people and common situations, a deliberate departure from the heroic or extraordinary narratives that characterized much of the earlier Indian English fiction [1, p. 1]. Singh's characters are not larger-than-life figures but villagers, clerks, moneylenders, and petty criminals, individuals whose lives are shaped by forces they barely comprehend. The Chief Minister of Andhra Pradesh, speaking at the award ceremony where Singh received the "Honest Man of the Year Award" in 2000, described him as a "humorous writer and incorrigible believer in human goodness with a devil-may-care attitude and a courageous mind" [1, p. 1; 3, p. 1]. This characterization captures the paradoxical quality of Singh's humanism: it is simultaneously optimistic about human potential and clear-eyed about human failings, capable of humor in the face of tragedy and courage in the face of oppression.

Singh's approach to Partition literature is distinguished by its refusal to assign blame to any single community or political faction. In *Train to Pakistan*, the novelist presents the violence of 1947 as a collective failure of humanity rather than the fault of Hindus, Muslims, or Sikhs alone. As one critic notes, "Muslims said the Hindus had planned and started the killing. According to the Hindus the Muslims were to blame. The fact is, both sides killed" [8, p. 2]. This even-handedness, which some contemporaries criticized as moral relativism, represents a deliberate artistic and philosophical choice. Singh understood that the Partition violence could not be reduced to a simple narrative of perpetrators and victims; instead, it required a more nuanced representation that acknowledged the complicity of all communities in the cycle of revenge and counter-revenge. This perspective aligns with the novelist's broader commitment to what might be termed a secular humanism, one that transcends religious and communal boundaries in its affirmation of shared human dignity. The novel's famous concluding sequence, in which the Sikh protagonist Jugga sacrifices himself to prevent the massacre of Muslims on a train bound for Pakistan, embodies this humanistic vision: individual moral action, however futile in the face of historical catastrophe, retains its redemptive power.

The narrative technique that Singh employs in *Train to Pakistan* reflects his journalistic background and his commitment to realistic representation. The novel adopts a straightforward, almost documentary style, avoiding the experimental narrative strategies that characterized the work of many of his contemporaries [9, p. 1]. This stylistic choice is not merely aesthetic but ideological: Singh believed that the horror of Partition required a transparent narrative form that would not obscure the reality of violence through literary artifice. As one scholar observes, "realistic portrayal aims at a reportorial account of the experiences of individuals," and Singh's novel exemplifies this commitment to verisimilitude [9, p. 1]. The village of Mano Majra, with its railway station, its temple, its mosque, and its gurdwara, is rendered with meticulous attention to sensory detail, creating a world that feels tangible and lived-in. This realism serves a dual purpose: it grounds the novel's political and philosophical themes in concrete human experience, and it creates a powerful contrast between the ordinary rhythms of village life and the extraordinary violence that disrupts them. The train, which gives the novel its title, functions as both a literal vehicle of transportation and a symbol of the forces that sweep individuals up in historical currents beyond their control.

# 1.3 Introduction to the Novel *Train to Pakistan*

Khushwant Singh's *Train to Pakistan* (1956) stands as one of the most significant literary engagements with the Partition of India, offering a concentrated and unflinching examination of communal violence and its human cost. Originally published under the title *Mano Majra*, the novel represents Singh's first foray into fiction and draws extensively upon his personal experience as a witness to the cataclysmic events of 1947 [1, p. 2]. The work occupies a distinctive position within Partition literature, not only for its chronological proximity to the events it depicts but also for its deliberate focus on a single, isolated village as a microcosm of the subcontinental tragedy. Through this circumscribed setting, Singh achieves a narrative intensity that universalizes the particular, transforming the experiences of a handful of villagers into a profound meditation on the fragility of human community in the face of political manipulation and collective fear.

The novel is set in the fictional village of Mano Majra, a small settlement situated precisely on the newly demarcated border between India and Pakistan [5, p. 1]. This geographical positioning is central to the novel's dramatic structure, as the village that had existed for centuries in peaceful obscurity suddenly finds itself at the epicentre of history's most violent convulsion. Singh describes Mano Majra as a place where Hindus, Sikhs, and Muslims had lived together harmoniously "since times immemorial," sharing not only physical space but also cultural practices, economic interdependence, and social bonds that transcended religious identity [7, p. 1]. The village possesses a railway station, a small temple, a mosque, and a gurdwara, all situated in close proximity, symbolizing the coexistence that had characterized rural Punjab for generations. This initial portrayal of communal harmony is not merely nostalgic but serves a crucial narrative function: it establishes the baseline of normalcy against which the subsequent descent into violence can be measured. As one critic observes, the novel's power derives precisely from its depiction of how "the brotherly comradeship between the villagers is gone, and they are ready to eradicate the 'other' group" [6, p. 1].

The narrative action of *Train to Pakistan* unfolds over a few weeks in August and September of 1947, a period that coincides with the formal transfer of power and the eruption of widespread communal violence across the Punjab region [7, p. 1]. Singh structures the novel into four distinct parts—"Dacoity," "Kalyug," "Mano Majra," and "Karma"—each of which marks a progressive stage in the village's moral disintegration [1, p. 2]. The first section introduces the established order of Mano Majra and the initial disruption caused by a dacoity (robbery) that brings outside attention to the village. The second section, titled "Kalyug" or "the age of darkness" in Hindu cosmology, signals the encroachment of historical forces beyond the villagers' control. The third section deepens the focus on the community's internal dynamics as external pressures intensify, while the final section, "Karma," brings the narrative to its tragic resolution. This structural progression mirrors the novel's thematic arc: from innocence to complicity, from peace to violence, and from communal solidarity to atomized fear.

The title of the novel itself carries profound symbolic weight. The train functions as a multivalent symbol, representing simultaneously the mass exodus of refugees, the mechanical indifference of history, and the movement of people toward uncertain destinies [1, p. 2]. When one speaks of Partition, the image of trains overflowing with desperate humanity immediately comes to mind; the train thus becomes an emblem of the largest forced migration in human history, during which millions crossed the newly created borders in search of safety [2, p. 1]. Yet the train in Singh's novel also carries darker connotations. It is the vehicle through which violence arrives in Mano Majra, as news of massacres on trains traveling between India and Pakistan reaches the village and gradually poisons its communal atmosphere. The train that ultimately becomes the target of a planned massacre represents the culmination of this trajectory: the instrument of salvation transformed into an object of destruction. As one scholar notes, the title "hints at the reason of chaos which began in the novel," encapsulating the central irony of Partition—that the very mechanism of escape became a site of unimaginable horror [1, p. 2].

Singh populates Mano Majra with a carefully constructed ensemble of characters whose interactions illuminate the novel's central concerns. The most significant figure is Juggut Singh, commonly known as Jugga, a young Sikh of low social standing who is regarded by the village as a petty criminal and troublemaker. Jugga's romantic relationship with Nooran, a Muslim girl from the same village, forms the emotional core of the novel. Their love affair, conducted in secret and threatened by the rigid social boundaries of village life, becomes increasingly precarious as communal tensions escalate. Jugga is portrayed as a figure of raw, untamed energy, a man whose loyalty to Nooran transcends the religious categories that come to dominate the village's consciousness. His character arc culminates in a final act of sacrifice that stands as the novel's most powerful assertion of humanistic values against the tide of communal hatred [3, p. 1]. As one analysis observes, Jugga's decision to cut the rope meant to derail the train carrying Muslim refugees represents a moment of moral redemption: "His love for Nooran motivates him to act, transcending religious identity" [3, p. 1].

Nooran, though less fully developed than Jugga, functions as a crucial figure whose vulnerability exposes the gendered dimensions of Partition violence. Her silent terror and eventual separation from Jugga symbolize how "personal relationships are destroyed by communal boundaries imposed overnight" [3, p. 1]. Nooran's fate is emblematic of the disproportionate suffering borne by women during Partition, as their bodies became sites of communal honour and revenge. The novel strongly implies the vulnerability of women through fear and displacement, even if it does not dwell excessively on graphic sexual violence [3, p. 1]. The forced evacuation of Muslim women and children from Mano Majra underscores this theme, demonstrating how the political abstractions of nationhood translated into concrete, bodily trauma for the most vulnerable members of the community.

# 1.4 Introduction to the Film Adaptation

The cinematic adaptation of Khushwant Singh's *Train to Pakistan*, directed by Pamela Rooks and released in 1998, represents a significant attempt to translate the novel's complex meditation on Partition violence and humanism into the visual medium. The adaptation process, occurring over four decades after the novel's initial publication in 1956, necessitated substantial decisions regarding narrative condensation, character development, and thematic emphasis that fundamentally shape the film's relationship to its source text [7, p. 1]. As Khalid Alqadhi observes, adaptation constitutes "a rewriting of a story, which can't be a copy paste of the original" but rather represents "a subjective interpretation of the director, inspired by a text, and then crafting his film differently" [1, p. 5]. This section examines the film adaptation as both a faithful rendering of Singh's central concerns and an independent artistic work that necessarily transforms the novel's literary strategies into cinematic language.

The transition from literary text to filmic representation presents inherent challenges that Rooks and her production team confronted through deliberate creative choices. Alqadhi and Pawar note that "some concepts seem so difficult to be shifted into films as they bear so tragic, melancholic, or adventurous circumstances," requiring "paying more endless endeavors and time" to achieve effective visualization [7, p. 1]. The novel's depiction of the evacuation chaos—the transformation of love into hatred, peace into war, coexistence into arbitrary displacement—demanded careful translation into visual and auditory registers that could convey the emotional and historical weight of the Partition experience [7, p. 1]. Rooks and her fellow-actors, including Nirmal Pandey who played Jugga, undertook the construction of an imaginative village to represent Singh's Mano Majra, populating it with "hosts of people, pets, and simple mud huts" to recreate the pastoral environment that the novel describes in meticulous detail [9, p. 2]. This physical reconstruction of the novel's setting demonstrates the adaptation's commitment to visual authenticity while simultaneously revealing the interpretive labor required to materialize literary description.

The film maintains substantial fidelity to the novel's central themes, setting, and key narrative events, establishing a clear continuity between the two works. Both the novel and the film are set in the village of Mano Majra on the Indo-Pakistan border, depicting a community initially characterized by interreligious harmony that becomes progressively torn apart by the forces of Partition [3, p. 1]. The central characters remain consistent across both versions: Jugga Singh, a Sikh outlaw, and Nooran, a Muslim girl, whose love affair transcends communal boundaries and embodies the human cost of political division [3, p. 1]. The film preserves several pivotal events from the novel, including the murder of Lala Ramlal, the dacoity committed by Mali and his gang, the subsequent framing of Jugga and Iqbal, the arrival of the ghost train carrying massacred bodies, and Jugga's tragic sacrifice to prevent the train's destruction [3, p. 1]. The symbolism of the train itself—initially representing British imperialism before transforming into an emblem of Partition's carnage—operates powerfully in both versions, with the ghost train serving as "a pivotal symbol of the communal violence that disrupts this idyllic life" [3, p. 1]. The realistic portrayal of violence, particularly the depiction of mutilated bodies, closely follows the novel's descriptions, with Rooks emphasizing "the brutal realities of Partition" through visual strategies such as the close-up of Imam Baksh's fearful expression as he anticipates the train's arrival [3, p. 1].

Despite these fundamental similarities, the film necessarily condenses and streamlines certain aspects of the novel for cinematic purposes, resulting in significant differences in narrative scope and character complexity. The novel's expansive treatment of time and character backstories gives way in the film to a condensed timeline that focuses more intensely on the central conflict and the immediate urgency surrounding the Partition [3, p. 1]. # 1.5 Aim and Objectives of the Study

The present study undertakes a critical examination of the representation of Partition violence and the articulation of humanism in Khushwant Singh's *Train to Pakistan*, with the overarching aim of demonstrating how the novel negotiates the tension between communal fragmentation and the persistence of humanist values. While existing scholarship has addressed the historical dimensions of the Partition and its literary representations, a sustained analysis of the dialectical relationship between violence and humanism within Singh's narrative framework remains insufficiently explored. The aim of this study, therefore, is to interrogate the ways in which *Train to Pakistan* simultaneously depicts the brutal realities of communal violence and gestures toward a transcendent humanism that resists the logic of religious and national division. This aim is operationalised through a series of interconnected objectives that collectively advance an argument about the novel's ethical and literary significance.

The first objective of this study is to analyze the representation of Partition violence in *Train to Pakistan* and its profound human cost. Singh's novel does not merely document the historical events of 1947 but rather dramatises the visceral, psychological, and social dimensions of violence as it disrupts the fabric of rural life in Mano Majra. The narrative presents violence not as an abstract historical force but as a lived experience that fundamentally alters human relationships, community structures, and individual psyches. As one study observes, the novel's characters "categorize themselves and others based on religious identities" in ways that "provide a framework for understanding their own identity and distinguishing themselves from others" [8, p. 5]. This categorisation, however, is not a neutral cognitive process but one that becomes increasingly violent as the Partition unfolds. The study will examine how Singh portrays the escalation from latent communal tension to explicit brutality, attending to the specific scenes of massacre, abduction, and destruction that punctuate the narrative. By foregrounding the human cost of violence—the loss of life, the fracturing of families, the erosion of trust—Singh compels readers to confront the ethical implications of communal hatred. This objective thus establishes the foundation for understanding the novel's critique of violence as a mode of social and political organisation.

The second objective seeks to examine the construction of identity and belonging within the novel through the lens of Social Identity Theory. Drawing upon the theoretical framework developed by Henri Tajfel and John Turner, this study will investigate how characters in *Train to Pakistan* negotiate their identities in relation to religious, cultural, and national affiliations [8, p. 5]. The novel presents a complex landscape of identity formation in which characters simultaneously inhabit multiple subject positions—as villagers, as members of religious communities, as subjects of an emerging nation-state—and must navigate the conflicting demands of these positions. The dialogue between Juggut Singh and Hukum Chand exemplifies this tension: when Juggut Singh protests, "I am a Sikh. Why do they call me a Muslim? Is it because I wear a beard and don't cut my hair? I have always been a Sikh. It's just that nobody knows it," Hukum Chand responds, "It doesn't matter what you are. The fact is, you are an outcast" [2, p. 6]. This exchange reveals the gap between self-identification and external ascription, demonstrating how identity becomes a site of contestation during periods of social upheaval. The study will analyze how Singh portrays the processes of categorization, social comparison, and identification that Tajfel and Turner describe, attending to the ways in which characters align themselves with in-groups and discriminate against out-groups [8, p. 5]. This objective is crucial for understanding how the novel critiques the reduction of human complexity to religious labels and the violence that such reduction enables.

The third objective is to evaluate the novel's articulation of humanism as a counter-narrative to communal violence. While *Train to Pakistan* does not offer a simplistic or sentimental vision of human unity, it nonetheless gestures toward a humanist ethics that transcends religious and national divisions. This humanism is not abstract or universalist in a naive sense but is rather grounded in the specificities of place, community, and embodied experience. The character of Malli articulates this vision when he tells refugees seeking safety, "There is no India anymore, brother. There is no Pakistan. There is only Mano Majra" [2, p. 6]. This statement does not deny the reality of political division but instead insists on the primacy of local, human connections over abstract national identities. Similarly, the dialogue between Iqbal and Nooran reveals the tension between individual humanist belief and social reality: Iqbal declares, "Religion doesn't matter to me, Nooran. I see you as a person, not as a Sikh or a Muslim," to which Nooran responds, "But the world sees us differently, Iqbal. We cannot ignore the reality around us" [2, p. 6]. This exchange encapsulates the central ethical dilemma of the novel: the desire to see beyond religious labels confronts the social and political forces that insist upon those labels. The study will examine how Singh navigates this dilemma, analyzing the moments of human connection—between Juggut Singh and Nooran, between Iqbal and the villagers—that resist the logic of communal division. This objective thus addresses the novel's affirmative dimension, demonstrating that *Train to Pakistan* is not merely a chronicle of violence but also a testament to the persistence of humanist values.

# 1.6 Research Methodology and Scope

The research methodology employed in this study proceeds from a comparative textual and film analysis that interrogates the relationship between Khushwant Singh’s novel *Train to Pakistan* (1956) and Pamela Rooks’s cinematic adaptation (1998). The methodological framework integrates close reading, thematic analysis, and comparative adaptation studies to examine how both works represent the violence of the Partition and articulate a humanist response to communal hatred. This approach enables the study to move beyond mere documentation of narrative differences toward a sustained interpretive engagement with the ideological, aesthetic, and ethical dimensions of each work [1, p. 5]. By situating the analysis within the broader context of Partition literature and cinema, the methodology facilitates a nuanced understanding of how humanism functions as both a thematic concern and a formal principle across the two mediums.

The study employs a qualitative research design that prioritizes interpretive depth over quantitative generalization. Close reading constitutes the primary analytical tool for examining the novel, focusing on character development, narrative structure, and the symbolic dimensions of key episodes and dialogues [2, p. 2]. This method allows for the identification of recurring motifs—such as the train, the river, and the village of Mano Majra—that operate as sites of meaning production within the text. The analysis attends to how Singh constructs the psychological interiority of characters like Juggut Singh, Iqbal, and Hukum Chand, tracing the ways in which their individual struggles mirror the larger communal crisis. Such an approach recognizes that literary texts generate meaning through formal and stylistic choices, not merely through plot or subject matter [3, p. 6].

The film analysis proceeds from a similar methodological orientation, albeit adapted to the specific demands of the cinematic medium. The study examines Rooks’s adaptation through the lens of adaptation theory, which posits that filmic adaptations are interpretative acts rather than transparent reproductions of source texts [5, p. 5]. This theoretical framework acknowledges that directors necessarily condense, expand, and reorder narrative elements to suit the visual and temporal constraints of cinema. Consequently, the analysis compares the novel and film across several axes: character development, narrative emphasis, symbolic imagery, and the deployment of music and visual aesthetics [6, p. 2]. The song “Sanu Aa Milya Yaar Piyaariya,” for instance, receives particular attention as a sonic marker of pre-Partition harmony that the novel cannot replicate through prose alone [6, p. 2]. This comparative dimension enriches the study by revealing how meaning shifts across mediums while remaining anchored to the same humanist core.

Data collection proceeds through a systematic selection of primary and secondary sources. The primary corpus consists of Singh’s novel and Rooks’s film, supplemented by archival materials such as interviews with the director and critical essays on both works [4, p. 2]. Secondary sources encompass scholarly monographs, journal articles, and literary criticism that address Partition literature, adaptation studies, and the work of Khushwant Singh. The study also draws upon theoretical texts concerning humanism, violence, and identity to provide the conceptual vocabulary necessary for analysis [1, p. 5]. This multi-source approach ensures that the study is grounded in established scholarship while also offering original interpretive contributions.

Thematic analysis serves as the principal method for organizing and interpreting the collected data. Following the procedures outlined by previous scholars, the study codes textual and filmic evidence into thematic categories that reflect the research objectives: the representation of violence, the articulation of humanist values, and the negotiation of identity amidst communal conflict [3, p. 6]. These categories emerge from a preliminary reading of the sources and are refined through iterative analysis. For example, dialogues such as Iqbal’s assertion that “religion doesn’t matter to me” and Malli’s declaration that “there is only Mano Majra” provide evidence for the theme of transcendent human solidarity [3, p. 6]. The analysis interprets these moments not merely as narrative events but as ideological statements that reveal Singh’s and Rooks’s respective humanist commitments.

The comparative dimension of the methodology requires careful attention to both similarity and difference between the novel and film. While the adaptation remains largely faithful to the central plot and thematic concerns of the source text, significant divergences emerge in character portrayal and narrative emphasis [6, p. 2]. The novel presents Iqbal as a cynical intellectual who questions the efficacy of political solutions, whereas the film depicts him as a more straightforward idealist driven by social reform [6, p. 2]. Similarly, Hukum Chand’s moral complexity—his struggle between duty and justice—is diminished in the film, which portrays him primarily as a helpless bureaucrat [6, p. 2]. These differences are not treated as failures of adaptation but as interpretative choices that reflect the constraints and possibilities of the cinematic medium. The analysis argues that such changes, while simplifying certain character arcs, enable the film to foreground the emotional immediacy of the climactic sacrifice, thereby reinforcing the humanist message through affective rather than intellectual means [7, p. 7].

Ethical considerations inform every stage of the research process, given the sensitive nature of the Partition and its ongoing political resonance. The analysis proceeds with respect for the historical suffering of affected communities, avoiding sensationalism or reductive judgments about communal identities [1, p. 6]. The study acknowledges that both Singh and Rooks grappled with representing extreme violence while also affirming human dignity, and the methodology follows their example by balancing critical analysis with ethical responsibility. No attempt is made to offer definitive moral verdicts on the actions of characters, nor to impose contemporary political perspectives onto historical events. Instead, the study seeks to illuminate the ways in which literature and cinema can facilitate reflection on the conditions of human coexistence in the aftermath of catastrophe.

The scope of the study is delimited by several parameters. First, the analysis focuses exclusively on Singh’s *Train to Pakistan* and Rooks’s adaptation, without extending to other works by Singh or other cinematic representations of the Partition. This narrow focus allows for depth of analysis while acknowledging that broader comparative work remains for future scholarship. Second, the study does not engage in a comprehensive survey of Partition historiography, though it draws upon historical context where necessary to situate the literary and filmic analysis. Third, the methodological emphasis on humanism does not preclude attention to other thematic concerns—such as gender, class, and nationalism—but subordinates these to the central interpretive framework. The study thus maintains coherence while also recognizing the richness of the source materials.

Validity and reliability are ensured through multiple strategies. A detailed research journal documents the analytical process, including decisions about coding and interpretation, thereby providing transparency and enabling the tracking of potential biases [3, p. 6]. The analysis draws upon established theoretical frameworks that have been validated through prior scholarship, including adaptation theory and thematic analysis. Each interpretive claim is supported by textual or filmic evidence, minimizing the risk of unsubstantiated assertion [2, p. 2]. The study also engages with divergent scholarly opinions, acknowledging alternative readings while defending its own interpretive choices through reasoned argument. This commitment to scholarly rigor ensures that the conclusions are both defensible and open to critical engagement.

The methodology thus provides a coherent framework for investigating how both novel and film articulate a humanist vision in the face of Partition violence. By combining close reading, thematic analysis, and comparative adaptation study, the research proceeds from the premise that literary and cinematic texts do not merely reflect historical events but actively shape the ethical imagination of their audiences. The following chapter, Chapter Two, turns to a detailed analysis of the novel’s representation of violence, examining how Singh constructs the physical and psychological dimensions of communal hatred before tracing the countervailing forces of love, sacrifice, and human solidarity.

# 1.7 Thesis Statement

Khushwant Singh’s *Train to Pakistan* constitutes a seminal literary intervention in representing the violence of the Partition of India, yet the novel simultaneously performs a complex negotiation between documenting historical atrocity and affirming the possibility of humanist transcendence. While existing scholarship has extensively examined the novel’s portrayal of communal violence and its historical verisimilitude, the critical literature has insufficiently addressed the specific narrative strategies through which Singh constructs a humanist critique of sectarian identity. This dissertation argues that *Train to Pakistan* employs a tripartite narrative architecture—comprising satirical defamiliarization, the subversion of communal binaries through characterological complexity, and the symbolic reclamation of moral agency—to articulate a humanist vision that neither denies the reality of collective violence nor capitulates to its logic. Through this framework, the novel demonstrates that the humanist impulse, far from being a naive universalism, emerges precisely from the confrontation with atrocity and the refusal to reduce human beings to their communal affiliations.

The existing critical discourse has established that Singh’s work engages decisively with the trauma of Partition. Scholars have noted that the novel “vividly portrays the communal violence and human suffering during the partition” [10, p. 6] and that Singh “fearlessly addressed controversial and taboo subjects in his writing, challenging societal norms and promoting open dialogue on social issues” [1, p. 15]. However, the dominant critical tendency has been to read the novel primarily as a documentary representation of historical events, emphasizing its testimonial function while neglecting the formal and philosophical dimensions of its humanist argument. Studies that apply Social Identity Theory to the novel have illuminated how characters “categorize themselves and others based on religious identities” and engage in processes of “in-group favouritism and out-group discrimination” [6, p. 5], yet these analyses tend to foreground the deterministic power of social categorization without adequately accounting for the novel’s countervailing gestures toward individual moral agency and cross-communal solidarity.

This dissertation intervenes in this critical lacuna by proposing that Singh’s narrative operates on multiple registers simultaneously. The first of these registers is satirical defamiliarization, a mode that Singh deploys to unsettle the reader’s conventional expectations and to expose the absurdity underlying communal divisions. Criticism has acknowledged that Singh’s “use of humor and satire is a prominent aspect of his writing style” and that he “employed wit and irony to comment on various social, political, and cultural aspects of Indian society” [10, p. 6]. This study argues, however, that satire in *Train to Pakistan* is not merely a stylistic ornament but a structurally essential device for creating critical distance from the very categories of identity that produce violence. By juxtaposing the mundane absurdities of village life with the encroaching horror of communal massacre, Singh compels readers to recognize the artificiality of the boundaries that Partition imposes. The satire functions to denaturalize sectarian identity, revealing it as a construct rather than an essence, and thereby opens a space for alternative modes of affiliation grounded in shared human vulnerability.

The second register of Singh’s humanist argument consists of the systematic subversion of communal binaries through the creation of characters whose identities resist reduction to religious or national categories. The novel’s protagonist, Juggut Singh, exemplifies this complexity; as one analysis notes, his dialogue reveals a “struggle with his identity and how his appearance and actions have led to him being labelled as something he is not” [3, p. 6]. Juggut Singh cannot be contained within the neat taxonomies of Sikh, Muslim, or Hindu that the Partition logic demands, and his ultimate act of sacrificial heroism—rescuing a Muslim girl despite his own impending execution—embodies a humanist morality that transcends communal calculation. Similarly, the character of Iqbal articulates a vision of identity that refuses sectarian reduction, declaring that “religion doesn’t matter” and that he sees individuals “as a person, not as a Sikh or a Muslim” [3, p. 6]. Yet the novel does not simplistically celebrate this position; as Nooran’s response makes clear, such individualism operates against the grain of a social reality in which religious identity determines survival [3, p. 6]. The novel thus stages a dialectical tension between the aspiration toward universal humanity and the crushing weight of historical circumstance.

The third register of analysis concerns the novel’s symbolic reclamation of moral agency within conditions of collective violence. Critical accounts have rightly emphasized the novel’s documentary power in representing how communal identities intensify during periods of crisis, as characters “aligned themselves with their respective communities for protection and survival” and “the rigid boundaries between in-groups and out-groups result in the exclusion of individuals from other communities” [6, p. 5]. This dissertation contends, however, that the novel’s most significant achievement lies in its depiction of characters who resist this determinism and who reclaim the capacity for ethical choice. The character of Malli articulates this alternative when he declares to arriving refugees that “there is no India anymore, brother. There is no Pakistan. There is only Mano Majra” [3, p. 6]. This statement does not represent a naive denial of political reality but rather an affirmation of a local, embodied, and relational form of belonging that precedes and potentially supersedes the abstract categories of nation and religion. The novel suggests that humanism, understood not as a philosophical doctrine but as a practice of recognition and care, can survive even the most extreme conditions of dehumanization.

This dissertation further argues that the film adaptation of *Train to Pakistan* constitutes a distinct interpretive act that transforms the novel’s humanist argument in significant ways. The adaptation, by virtue of its medium, must translate Singh’s narrative strategies into visual and auditory registers, foregrounding the affective dimensions of human suffering and solidarity. However, the adaptation also risks diluting the novel’s satirical edge and its nuanced characterological complexity, potentially substituting sentimentality for the more ambivalent humanism that the literary text sustains. By examining the relationship between novel and film, this study illuminates how the humanist vision undergoes translation across media, revealing both the possibilities and the limitations of each form.

The conceptual framework that guides this analysis draws upon critical humanist thought, which emphasizes the capacity for ethical agency, the value of individual dignity, and the possibility of cross-cultural understanding without erasing difference. This framework does not posit a universal human essence that transcends historical specificity but rather insists on the ethical imperative to recognize the humanity of others even—and especially—when social structures conspire to deny that recognition. In this respect, the dissertation aligns with scholarship that reads Singh’s work as demonstrating that “his writings continue to inspire and provoke thought, making him an enduring figure in the literary canon” [1, p. 15]. The humanism that emerges from *Train to Pakistan* is not a pre-existing philosophical commitment that the novel simply illustrates but rather a hard-won achievement that must be wrested from the very violence that threatens to annihilate it.

The significance of this argument extends beyond the interpretation of a single text. By demonstrating how *Train to Pakistan* negotiates the tension between historical determinism and individual moral agency, this dissertation contributes to broader debates about the capacity of literature to address collective trauma without reducing human beings to victims or perpetrators. Singh’s novel offers a model for representing violence that neither aestheticizes suffering nor retreats into cynical despair, and its formal strategies provide resources for thinking about how literature might articulate humanist values in contexts of extreme social division. The dissertation thus responds to the critical need for readings that attend simultaneously to the historical specificity of Partition literature and to the philosophical questions that such literature raises about the nature of humanity, community, and ethical responsibility.

In pursuing this argument, the dissertation proceeds through a series of structured analyses. The following chapter establishes the historical and critical context necessary for understanding Singh’s humanist project, situating *Train to Pakistan* within the broader field of Partition literature and examining how the novel both participates in and departs from this tradition. Subsequent chapters examine each of the three narrative registers identified above—satirical defamiliarization, characterological complexity, and symbolic moral agency—before turning to a comparative analysis of the novel and its film adaptation. The conclusion synthesizes these analyses to articulate the novel’s distinctive contribution to humanist thought and to consider the implications of this argument for the study of literature and violence more broadly. Through this comprehensive investigation, the dissertation aims to demonstrate that *Train to Pakistan* remains a vital text not only for its historical testimony but for its profound and unsettling meditation on what it means to be human in the aftermath of atrocity.
