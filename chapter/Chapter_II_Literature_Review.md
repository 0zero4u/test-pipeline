# Chapter II — Review of Literature

# 2.1 Overview of Partition Literature

The literature of the Indian Partition constitutes one of the most significant bodies of postcolonial writing in the twentieth century, emerging directly from the cataclysmic events of 1947 that accompanied the end of British colonial rule. The Partition of the Indian subcontinent into the independent nations of India and Pakistan resulted in one of the largest and most violent mass migrations in human history, with approximately seventeen million people displaced across newly drawn borders and an incalculable number of deaths [1, p. 2]. This historical rupture generated a substantial and enduring literary response, as writers sought to document, interpret, and bear witness to the human tragedy that unfolded. The development of Partition literature reflects the complex interplay between historical documentation and imaginative reconstruction, as creative writers assumed the responsibility of representing experiences that official historiography often elided or simplified [2, p. 1]. Understanding the evolution, thematic preoccupations, and critical debates surrounding this literary corpus provides essential context for examining Khushwant Singh's *Train to Pakistan* and its subsequent film adaptation.

The historical circumstances of Partition produced a literature that grappled simultaneously with collective trauma and individual suffering. As Settar and Gupta observe, the violence that accompanied Partition was so extensive that its precise human cost remains unknowable [1, p. 2]. Riots, mass murders, abductions, and sexual violence became widespread, shattering the communal harmony that had characterized many regions of the subcontinent for centuries. The unplanned and hasty manner in which political leaders executed the division exacerbated the tragedy, leaving ordinary citizens to bear the consequences of decisions made at the highest levels of power [1, p. 2]. In response to this historical catastrophe, Partition literature emerged not merely as a record of events but as a mode of understanding the psychological, moral, and social dimensions of the rupture. N.S. Gundur argues that fictional narratives of Partition, while engaging with politico-historical realities, also function as critiques of political history, offering insights into the human dimensions of political decisions that conventional historical accounts cannot capture [1, p. 2]. This dual function—documentary and interpretive—characterizes the genre and distinguishes it from both official historiography and purely imaginative fiction.

The development of Partition literature as a distinct field of study has been shaped by the contributions of numerous writers and scholars working across languages, genres, and ideological positions. Among the most significant early works in English is Khushwant Singh's *Train to Pakistan* (1956), widely recognized as the first novel to address the theme of Partition directly [5, p. 1]. Singh's novel established a template for subsequent Partition fiction by focusing on a microcosmic community—the fictional village of Mano Majra—whose harmonious existence is destroyed by the forces of communal violence and political division [7, p. 3]. The novel's representation of a village where Sikhs and Muslims lived as brothers, only to be torn apart by events beyond their control, exemplifies the genre's preoccupation with the destruction of ordinary life by historical forces [5, p. 2]. Alongside Singh, writers such as Bhisham Sahni, whose Hindi novel *Tamas* (1974) offers a searing portrayal of communal violence, and Saadat Hasan Manto, whose Urdu short stories capture the absurdity and horror of Partition with devastating precision, have become canonical figures in the field [3, p. 1]. More recently, scholars such as Urvashi Butalia, whose oral history *The Other Side of Silence: Voices from the Partition of India* (2000) recovered the experiences of women, children, and other marginalized voices, have expanded the scope of Partition studies beyond the literary text to encompass testimonial and archival materials [4, p. 9; 10, p. 1]. This scholarly turn toward subaltern and feminist perspectives has fundamentally reshaped understandings of what Partition literature encompasses and whose stories it tells.

The major themes that animate Partition literature reflect the multifaceted nature of the historical experience itself. Violence and its aftermath constitute the most immediate and pervasive concern, with writers representing both the physical brutality of communal riots and the psychological trauma that persisted long after the events themselves [11, p. 1]. The theme of displacement and migration recurs throughout the corpus, as characters are forced to leave ancestral homes and navigate the precarious status of refugeehood [11, p. 2]. Closely related to these concerns is the theme of communal harmony destroyed, as writers depict pre-Partition communities characterized by coexistence and mutual dependence, only to show how political manipulation and fear eroded these bonds [5, p. 2]. The humanistic dimension of Partition literature emerges through representations of love, friendship, and solidarity that transcend religious divisions, even as the narrative acknowledges the overwhelming force of communal hatred [7, p. 3]. Furthermore, as Joshi argues, the politics of affect—the ways in which emotions such as fear, shame, love, and hatred are mobilized and represented—constitutes a crucial analytical lens for understanding how Partition literature constructs ethical positions and aligns readers with particular communities [2, p. 1]. These affective dimensions reveal the ideological investments of writers and the ways in which literary texts participate in broader debates about nationhood, belonging, and responsibility.

Critical scholarship on Partition literature has increasingly attended to the ideological and historiographical dimensions of the genre. Beerendra Pandey has argued that Partition novels written in English before 1984 tend to follow the configuration of nationalist history, reproducing rather than challenging dominant narratives of the event [2, p. 1]. This observation raises important questions about the relationship between literary representation and historical truth, particularly regarding the ethical responsibilities of writers who address traumatic events. Joshi contends that Partition literature often exhibits an "ethical tilt" toward particular communities, with affects of love and hatred distributed unevenly along communal lines [3, p. 1]. Such affective politics, he argues, can result in a "prose of demonization and denunciation" of the Other, undermining the reconciliatory potential that Partition writing might otherwise possess [3, p. 1]. These critical debates underscore the contested nature of Partition memory and the ongoing struggle over how the event should be remembered, represented, and understood. The recognition that Partition literature is not a neutral record but an active participant in the construction of historical meaning has profound implications for how individual texts, including *Train to Pakistan*, are read and evaluated.

The relationship between Partition literature and historiography remains a central concern for scholars working in the field. Official nationalist historiography, whether Indian or Pakistani, has tended to celebrate independence while minimizing the violence and suffering that accompanied it [2, p. 1]. Creative writers, by contrast, have often been more willing to confront the full horror of Partition, representing the human cost of political decisions in ways that challenge celebratory narratives [1, p. 2]. However, as Joshi notes, even literary representations are not free from ideological bias; writers may unconsciously reproduce the assumptions of nationalist historiography or deliberately align themselves with particular communal positions [2, p. 1]. The feminist historiography pioneered by scholars such as Butalia, Menon, and Bhasin has been particularly important in recovering experiences that both official history and mainstream literature have marginalized, especially those of women who suffered sexual violence, abduction, and forced conversion

## 2.2 Critical Studies on *Train to Pakistan*

Scholarly interpretations of Khushwant Singh's *Train to Pakistan* have established the novel as a foundational text within Partition literature, yet critical consensus remains divided on several fundamental questions regarding its treatment of violence, nationalism, and identity. The novel has attracted sustained academic attention since its publication in 1956, with critics variously reading it as a realistic historical account, a humanistic meditation on communal conflict, or a text complicit in nationalist historiography. A comprehensive examination of this critical corpus reveals both the richness of existing scholarship and significant gaps that warrant further investigation, particularly concerning the relationship between the novel's narrative strategies and its subsequent film adaptation.

The most prominent strand of criticism addresses the novel's representation of violence and communal trauma. Numerous scholars position *Train to Pakistan* as an unflinching portrait of the brutalities that accompanied Partition, emphasising Singh's refusal to assign exclusive blame to any single community. One critic observes that the novel "pictures the brutal, realistic story of political hatred and of mass passions" during the Partition, noting how "human values were killed by the demons of the communal violence" [2]. This interpretation foregrounds Singh's opening assertion that "both sides killed. Both shot and stabbed and speared and clubbed. Both tortured. Both raped" [2, p. 1; 5, p. 1; 6, p. 1], reading this declaration as evidence of the author's commitment to balanced representation. Shovon similarly argues that Singh, as "a survivor of partition ensued violence," presents through Mano Majra "a fictional case story of the horror and divides inflicted on the general people because of a bad cut" [7, p. 1]. Such readings position the novel as a therapeutic exercise, with Gundur suggesting that Singh wrote "as a therapeutic exercise to overcome the pangs of partition" [10, p. 1].

However, a more theoretically sophisticated body of criticism questions whether the novel achieves the objectivity it claims. Pandey's analysis, drawing on Cowasjee's earlier observations, identifies patterns in Sikh-authored Partition fiction that complicate claims of impartiality. Cowasjee notes three characteristic elements: "the presence of Sikh protagonist, documentary authentication from newspaper reports, and the justification of Sikh brutality against the Muslims as counterviolence" [12, p. 2]. Pandey extends this analysis to identify a fourth pattern specific to *Train to Pakistan*: "a conscious foregrounding of Sikhs as civilized citizen vis-à-vis the barbaric Muslims through the configuration of the protagonist, Juggat Singh" [12, p. 2]. This critical intervention challenges the widespread characterisation of Singh as "a witness turned writer" who provides an objective account [10, p. 1]. Furthermore, Pandey cautions that Partition literature risks "reopening the old wounds" through its engagement with nationalist historiography, a concern that resonates with Morey's identification of Indian nationalism as "the main focus" of the novel [12, p. 2; 12, p. 2].

The question of identity formation during communal conflict constitutes a second major area of critical inquiry. Recent scholarship has applied Social Identity Theory to analyse how characters in the novel navigate their religious, cultural, and national affiliations. Saeed, Shafi, and Masood argue that "religious, cultural, and national affiliations significantly influence the characters' identities and sense of belonging," observing that "characters' identities are subject to external categorizations" while also noting "the characters' agency in negotiating their identities" [11, p. 1]. This theoretical framework illuminates how the novel dramatises the process by which peaceful coexistence gives way to polarisation. One study identifies several dimensions of identity crisis in the novel, including the forced alignment with nation-states, displacement and loss, cross-cultural relationships that "challenge the boundaries of identity," and the "fluidity with which individuals adapt to protect themselves" during violence [9, p. 9]. These analyses demonstrate that *Train to Pakistan* functions not merely as a historical document but as a complex exploration of how collective identities are constructed, eroded, and transformed under conditions of extreme social stress.

A third significant theme in the critical literature concerns the tension between communalism and humanism in Singh's narrative. Srivastava contends that the novel "presents a realistic and emotional portrayal of communal violence, human suffering, displacement, and the collapse of moral values" while simultaneously highlighting "the enduring power of humanity, sacrifice, love, and compassion amidst violence and chaos" [4, p. 1]. This reading identifies a redemptive countercurrent within the text, arguing that "even during extreme violence, some characters retain their moral integrity and demonstrate compassion and courage" [4, p. 1]. The novel's existentialist dimensions have also received attention, with Pushpraj Singh arguing that *Train to Pakistan* "stays distinguished as it does not treat usual themes of indiscriminate killings as the central theme," instead foregrounding "other associated issues arising from partition, especially the human dimensions of the gruesome happenings" [5, p. 1]. Such interpretations position the novel within a humanistic framework that transcends mere historical reportage, as Bhatnagar suggests that it is "not partition but what it gets associated with and what it becomes symbolic of that attracts the attention of the writer" [5, p. 1].

Scholars have also examined the novel's formal and symbolic dimensions. The train itself functions as a central organising symbol, representing "movement, of the mass exodus of people" and "carrying people of different communities and religion in one way" [2, p. 2]. **2.3 Studies on Violence and Communalism**

The scholarly analysis of communal conflict and brutality in Partition literature has established a substantial critical framework for understanding how fictional representations engage with historical trauma. Within this body of work, Khushwant Singh’s *Train to Pakistan* occupies a particularly significant position, as critics have examined the novel’s depiction of violence not merely as a reflection of historical events but as a complex literary intervention into questions of morality, social cohesion, and political responsibility. The critical discourse surrounding violence and communalism in the novel reveals a consistent preoccupation with the mechanisms through which peaceful coexistence disintegrates under the pressure of political manipulation and collective fear.

Critical scholarship has consistently foregrounded the historical context of British colonial policy as a foundational cause of communal division. Scholars argue that the British strategy of “divide and rule” systematically encouraged religious polarization, creating conditions in which political tensions between the Indian National Congress and the Muslim League could escalate into unprecedented violence [1, p. 1]. The announcement of Partition in August 1947 precipitated widespread communal riots across Punjab, Bengal, and Delhi, with trains carrying refugees becoming targets of attack and villages being systematically destroyed [1, p. 1]. This historical context is essential for understanding Singh’s literary project, as critics have emphasized that the author’s personal witnessing of Partition horrors directly shaped the novel’s representational strategies [1, p. 1]. The novel thus functions not merely as fiction but as what one scholar terms a “documentary sugar-coated with characters and a story,” a characterization that underscores the tension between factual witness and imaginative reconstruction [7, p. 2].

A central concern in the critical literature is the process by which communalism—defined as the elevation of religious or ethnic loyalty to the point of hostility toward other communities—operates as a destructive force within the novel [1, p. 1]. Scholars have examined how Singh portrays the gradual erosion of social harmony in Mano Majra, a village initially characterized by peaceful coexistence among Sikhs, Muslims, and Hindus. As one critic observes, the villagers are “not deeply concerned with politics or religious conflict” at the outset; they “share daily life, celebrate festivals together, and depend on one another economically and socially” [1, p. 1]. This representation of prelapsarian harmony serves a crucial analytical function: it establishes that communal violence is not an inevitable expression of natural antipathy but rather a manufactured phenomenon produced by external political forces. The novel thus challenges essentialist notions of religious conflict by demonstrating that ordinary people “do not naturally hate each other” [1, p. 1].

The arrival of the ghost train carrying corpses from Pakistan constitutes a pivotal moment in the novel’s representation of violence, and critics have devoted considerable attention to its symbolic and narrative significance. Singh’s description of the train—once a symbol of connection and movement transformed into “a harbinger of death”—marks the point at which abstract rumors of violence become undeniable reality [6, p. 1]. Scholars note that this moment instills fear and suspicion among the villagers, triggering a rapid psychological transformation in which former neighbors begin to view members of other religions with distrust [1, p. 1]. The critical consensus holds that communal violence in *Train to Pakistan* is portrayed not as spontaneous but as “systematically produced through fear and retaliation” [6, p. 1]. Singh captures the reciprocal nature of this fear: “The fact was that the Muslims were afraid. The Sikhs were afraid too,” a formulation that emphasizes how fear becomes the primary emotional driver, replacing empathy and reason [6, p. 1].

Furthermore, scholars have examined the economic dimensions of communal violence as depicted in the novel. Gyanendra Pandey’s analysis of Partition fiction, as engaged by subsequent critics, highlights how economic rivalries often underpin ostensibly religious conflict. In *Train to Pakistan*, the killing and looting that accompany communal violence are described as being guided by economic interest, with one critic noting that “religion is no consideration among buyers and sellers; profit is the overwhelming motif” [7, p. 2]. This economic reading complicates simplistic moral frameworks that attribute violence solely to religious hatred, suggesting instead that material interests and political manipulation intersect to produce communal conflict [7, p. 2]. Such analyses align with broader scholarly arguments that Partition violence cannot be understood without attending to the “vested interest in keeping the communal tension going” that various actors possessed [7, p. 2].

The novel’s treatment of trauma has also received substantial critical attention, with scholars drawing on trauma theory to illuminate the psychological consequences of violence. Cathy Caruth’s definition of trauma as “an overwhelming experience of sudden, or catastrophic events in which the response to the events occurs in the often delayed, and uncontrolled repetitive occurrences of hallucinations and other intrusive phenomena” provides a theoretical framework for understanding characters’ responses to violence [5, p. 5; 10, p. 181]. Critics have applied this framework to the character of Hukum Chand, the magistrate who witnesses the train full of dead bodies and experiences a “cold numbness” that ultimately deadens his emotions entirely [5, p. 5]. The horrible scene recurs in his mind as a traumatic intrusion, demonstrating how violence produces lasting psychological damage that extends beyond immediate physical harm [5, p. 5]. This attention to trauma underscores the novel’s concern with the long-term emotional and moral consequences of communal brutality.

Finally, critical scholarship has examined how Singh maintains a balanced and unprejudiced perspective despite the emotional intensity of his subject matter. Scholars note that the novel records violence committed by all communities: “Muslims said the Hindus had planned and started the killing. According to the Hindus, the Muslims were to blame. The fact is, both sides killed. Both shot and stabbed and speared and clubbed. Both tortured, both raped” [12, p. 1]. This refusal to assign exclusive blame to any single community distinguishes the novel from more partisan representations of Partition and has been identified as a key element of its moral complexity [12, p. 1]. The critical literature thus positions *Train to Pakistan* as a work that exposes the machinery of communal violence while simultaneously refusing to surrender to the logic of communal hatred.

The scholarly discourse on violence and communalism in *Train to Pakistan* establishes that the novel functions as both a historical document and a moral inquiry, examining how political manipulation, fear, and economic interests converge to destroy social harmony. This critical foundation prepares the ground for an examination of the novel’s humanist dimensions, to which the following section now turns.

## 2.4 Studies on Humanism and Moral Crisis

A significant body of critical scholarship examines Khushwant Singh’s *Train to Pakistan* through the lens of humanism and moral crisis, arguing that the novel offers a dual vision of human nature capable of both extreme cruelty and redemptive compassion. These studies collectively position the text not merely as a historical account of Partition violence but as a profound meditation on the survival of ethical values in circumstances of collective moral collapse. The critical discourse on humanism in the novel establishes a framework for understanding how individual acts of sacrifice and love function as counter-narratives to communal hatred, thereby providing a necessary foundation for examining the ethical dimensions of both the novel and its film adaptation.

The collapse of moral values during Partition constitutes a central preoccupation for scholars who read *Train to Pakistan* as a cautionary tale about the fragility of ethical order. Srivastava argues that the novel demonstrates how communal violence destroys not only lives but also “moral and cultural values,” as ordinary people who once lived peacefully together become victims of political manipulation and collective fear [1, p. 3]. This analysis emphasizes that the moral crisis depicted in the novel is not an abstract philosophical concern but a concrete social phenomenon: the gradual erosion of trust, compassion, and justice that once governed relationships between Sikhs, Muslims, and Hindus in the fictional village of Mano Majra. The critical scholarship consistently identifies the moment when the villagers, initially reluctant, become complicit in a plot to massacre Muslims on a train bound for Pakistan as the novel’s most disturbing illustration of moral corruption. Srivastava notes that Singh portrays these characters not as inherently evil but as individuals whose moral judgment has been overwhelmed by fear and revenge: “They were not bad men. They were not murderers. But they were afraid” [7, p. 1]. This reading suggests that the moral crisis of Partition is fundamentally a crisis of collective fear overriding individual conscience, a condition that Singh presents with restrained objectivity rather than moral condemnation.

Furthermore, scholars contend that the novel’s representation of moral survival through individual sacrifice serves as the counterpoint to this broader ethical collapse. Rashmi identifies in *Train to Pakistan* a “triumph of human spirit over communal violence,” arguing that humanistic values ultimately provide a framework for distinguishing right from wrong even in the most extreme circumstances [4, p. 1]. This critical perspective positions Jugga’s final act—cutting the rope meant to derail the train carrying his beloved Nooran and other Muslims to Pakistan—as the novel’s central affirmation of humanism. Chew similarly observes that Jugga’s “love for the Muslim girl Nooran and his attempt to save his beloved from the planned attack of Sikhs” culminates in a sacrifice that transcends religious identity and communal hatred [9, p. 3]. The critical scholarship thus interprets Jugga’s death not as a conventional heroic gesture but as an impulsive, deeply human act that asserts the possibility of moral agency within a context of overwhelming violence. As one analysis frames it, Singh writes with “restrained poignancy: ‘His body went limp, but the train went safely on to Pakistan,’” a moment that demonstrates how “humanity can survive even in the darkest times, though at a terrible cost” [7, p. 1]. This scholarly emphasis on redemptive sacrifice establishes humanism as the novel’s ethical counterweight to communal destruction.

Moreover, the critical literature consistently identifies the humanistic dimension of *Train to Pakistan* as a deliberate authorial strategy that distinguishes the novel from other Partition narratives. Singh contends that while many works of Partition literature focus on indiscriminate killings as their central theme, Singh’s novel “strikes a strong note of humanism” by foregrounding “other associated issues arising from partition, especially the human dimensions of the gruesome happenings” [3, p. 2]. This humanistic approach manifests in Singh’s refusal to assign blame to any single community, his focus on ordinary villagers rather than political leaders, and his insistence on the shared suffering of Sikhs, Muslims, and Hindus. Al-Qadhi and Pawar argue that the novel’s treatment of humanism extends to its exploration of how “love is going to lose its value, how family is going for endless social distancing, how religions failed to satisfy the human need, and even how lovers were forced to get rid of love and to replace it with the predicament of sacrifice” [5, p. 1]. This reading positions the novel’s humanism as simultaneously tragic and affirming: tragic because human relationships are destroyed by communal boundaries imposed overnight, but affirming because individual acts of love and sacrifice retain their power to transcend these boundaries. The critical consensus thus holds that *Train to Pakistan* offers a nuanced humanism that acknowledges the capacity for both cruelty and selflessness without resorting to sentimentalism or moral simplification.

In contrast, some scholars emphasize the structural and political dimensions of the novel’s moral crisis, arguing that the failure of state institutions and political leadership constitutes the deeper context for communal violence. Darlami Magar observes that the novel presents Mano Majra as “a perfect example of structural trauma,” a microcosm of secular harmony that is dismantled not by inherent communal animosity but by external political forces [11, p. 1]. Srivastava similarly contends that Singh criticizes political leaders and authorities for encouraging communal division, noting that characters like Magistrate Hukum Chand represent “the moral weakness of political leadership” and that “the government fails to protect innocent citizens and instead contributes to fear and confusion” [10, p. 2]. This scholarly perspective complicates the humanistic reading by situating individual moral choices within a broader context of administrative collapse and political manipulation. The novel’s treatment of the state’s failure—police officials who appear confused or indifferent, administrators whose guilt-ridden consciences remain ineffective—suggests that the moral crisis of Partition cannot be reduced to individual ethical failures but must be understood as the product of systemic breakdown. As one analysis emphasizes, “The machinery of the state had collapsed,” creating an administrative vacuum that enables communal violence to flourish unchecked [7, p. 1]. This critical dimension adds crucial complexity to the humanistic framework by demonstrating that individual acts of moral courage, however powerful, occur within a context of institutional failure that constrains their effectiveness.

## 2.5 Studies on Film Adaptation

The adaptation of literary texts into cinematic works constitutes a complex intermedial process that raises fundamental questions about fidelity, interpretation, and the distinct capacities of verbal and visual narrative forms. Film adaptation theory, as articulated by scholars such as Bluestone and Al-Qadhi, posits that the transformation of a written work into a filmic medium necessitates not merely a reproduction of content but a reimagining of narrative through the aesthetic possibilities of cinema. Khalid Al-Qadhi argues that adaptation constitutes "a rewriting of a story, which can't be a copy paste of the original," and represents "a subjective interpretation of the director, inspired by a text, and then crafting his film differently" [1, p. 5]. This theoretical position challenges earlier fidelity-based models of adaptation critique, which evaluated film versions primarily on their closeness to source texts, and instead emphasizes the productive, interpretive labor involved in cinematic translation.

The scholarly literature on film adaptation has increasingly recognized that the constraints and possibilities of the cinematic medium inevitably reshape source narratives. Al-Qadhi and Pawar observe that literary works often can be written within a year, whereas their film adaptations "at least took three years to be depicted into movies," demonstrating that "writing the work of art often is easier than transforming that work into the world of videoing" [5, p. 1]. The cinematic adaptation process involves decisions about pacing, visual emphasis, character development, and narrative condensation that reflect both the director's interpretive choices and the requirements of visual storytelling. As Goel notes, films attract a wider audience than literature as they "transcend barriers of religion, class and language," yet this broader reach comes at the cost of narrative complexity, as the cinematic medium must streamline and simplify [3, p. 1].

The adaptation of Khushwant Singh's *Train to Pakistan* into Pamela Rooks' 1998 film of the same name has generated significant scholarly attention as a case study in the possibilities and limitations of cinematic translation. The novel, published in 1956, narrates the devastating impact of Partition on the fictional village of Mano Majra, tracing the disintegration of communal harmony amid escalating violence and forced migration. Rooks' adaptation, produced over four decades after the novel's publication, attempts to translate Singh's literary vision into visual terms while remaining "largely faithful to the core themes and narrative of the novel" [3, p. 1]. This fidelity, however, is necessarily selective, as the film "inevitably condenses, streamlines, and emphasizes certain aspects for cinematic impact" [3, p. 1].

Critical analyses of the adaptation identify substantial areas of convergence between novel and film, particularly in setting, central characters, key events, and thematic concerns. Both works are set in the border village of Mano Majra and depict a community "initially depicted as living in harmony" that is "eventually torn apart by the forces of Partition" [9, p. 1]. The central love story between Jugga Singh, a Sikh outlaw, and Nooran, a Muslim girl, remains the emotional core of both narratives, and their relationship "reflects the human cost of the Partition" [9, p. 1]. Key plot events such as the murder of Lala Ramlal, the framing of Jugga and Iqbal, the arrival of the ghost train carrying massacred bodies, and Jugga's sacrificial act to prevent a train bombing are preserved across both media [9, p. 1]. The symbolism of the train itself operates similarly in both versions, functioning initially as a marker of British imperialism before transforming into "a symbol of the carnage and tragedy caused by the Partition" [9, p. 2].

Despite these substantial similarities, the scholarly literature documents important divergences between novel and film that illuminate the distinct representational strategies of literary and cinematic media. A primary difference concerns character development and narrative depth. Goel demonstrates that while the novel "provides a rich exploration of character motivations, the film simplifies some of these aspects for brevity" [7, p. 2]. Iqbal's political identity as a socialist is more subtly introduced in the film, and his internal conflicts regarding Partition violence are "not as thoroughly explored as in the novel" [7, p. 2]. The film presents him as "a more straightforward idealist," whereas the novel explores the limitations of his intellectual commitment [7, p. 2]. Similarly, Hukumchand's character is more complex in the novel, where "he grapples with his own moral dilemmas and the futility of his position," whereas the film "simplifies his character somewhat, focusing more on his role as a representative of authority" [7, p. 2]. These adaptations reflect the constraints of the cinematic medium and the necessity of condensing complex narratives for visual presentation.

The film also streamlines subplots, placing "greater emphasis on the central love story between Jugga and Nooran" while sidelining secondary characters such as the teenage prostitute Haseena, whose affair with Hukumchand is minimized [7, p. 2]. Furthermore, the film's treatment of narrative temporality differs significantly from the novel's. The film focuses on "the suspense and action surrounding the climax," whereas the novel "builds up to this conclusion more slowly, allowing the reader to reflect on the larger political and social implications of the events" [7, p. 2]. This shift from reflective to immediate temporality reflects fundamental differences between literary and cinematic modes of representation: the novel provides "more space for introspection and commentary on the Partition," while the film, constrained by its medium, "must focus on the immediacy of the events" [7, p. 2]. Al-Qadhi and Pawar affirm that the film adaptation can nonetheless "carry the fundamental theme in more apparent method than the written feat," suggesting that visual immediacy may offer its own representational advantages [5, p. 1].

The musical dimension of the film adaptation introduces an element absent from the novel. The song "Sanu Aa Milya Yaar Piyaariya," composed by Sai Bulleshah, "plays a significant role in the film, setting the tone for the peaceful existence of the villagers before the chaos of Partition" [7, p. 2]. This musical element serves as "a subtle narrative device to underscore the harmony that once existed in Mano Majra," whereas the novel relies on descriptive passages to capture the essence of village life [7, p. 2]. This addition exemplifies how cinematic adaptations can develop new affective registers unavailable to literary texts.

The comparative study of *Train to Pakistan* and its film adaptation reveals the interpretive labor involved in cinematic translation and the distinct representational capacities of verbal and visual media. # 2.6 Existing Scholarship on *Train to Pakistan* and Partition Narratives

The scholarly corpus on Khushwant Singh's *Train to Pakistan* and Partition narratives constitutes a substantial field of inquiry, yet significant gaps persist in comparative analysis between the novel and its cinematic adaptation. While individual studies have examined the novel through various critical lenses, and while film adaptation theory has developed sophisticated frameworks for analyzing literary-to-cinematic transformations, the intersection of these two domains—specifically, a systematic comparative analysis of Singh's novel and Pamela Rooks' 1998 film—remains underexplored. This section examines the existing scholarship, identifies the limitations in current approaches, and establishes the rationale for a more comprehensive comparative methodology.

Existing scholarship on *Train to Pakistan* has primarily addressed the novel as a historical document and a testament to Partition trauma. Chatterji positions the novel within a framework of counter-representation, arguing that Singh's work subverts mainstream nationalist narratives by disclosing "the horror of a forgotten past with both realism and bitterness" [8, p. 1]. This interpretation emphasizes the novel's function as a site of memory and contestation, wherein fiction becomes "almost the only social history we have of this time" [8, p. 1]. Such readings foreground the historiographical significance of the novel but do not extend their analytical frameworks to consider how the film adaptation either reinforces or dilutes this critical stance. The question of whether cinematic representation can sustain the same level of historiographical subversion remains unaddressed.

Furthermore, studies addressing communal violence and moral values in the novel have tended to focus exclusively on the literary text. Srivastava examines how Singh presents "both the destructive and redemptive aspects of human nature during Partition," analyzing characters, symbols, and social structures in conveying the novel's moral and political message [10, p. 1]. Similarly, Pushpraj Singh explores existentialist and humanistic dynamics within the novel, arguing that *Train to Pakistan* distinguishes itself from other Partition narratives by emphasizing "the human dimensions of the gruesome happenings" rather than treating indiscriminate killings as the central theme [12, p. 1]. These studies provide valuable insights into the novel's ethical framework but operate within a single-medium analysis, leaving unexplored the question of how the film adaptation translates, transforms, or potentially compromises these humanistic dimensions.

The comparative scholarship that does exist between the novel and the film remains preliminary and methodologically limited. Goel's analysis of "Similarities and Differences" between the two works catalogues points of convergence and divergence but does not develop a coherent interpretive framework for understanding the significance of these adaptations [2, p. 1]. The study acknowledges that Rooks' adaptation "inevitably condenses, streamlines, and emphasizes certain aspects for cinematic impact" [2, p. 1], yet it does not interrogate the ideological or aesthetic implications of these transformations. What is missing from such analyses is a critical engagement with the politics of adaptation—the choices made regarding which elements are preserved, which are modified, and which are omitted, and what these choices reveal about the respective capacities and constraints of literary and cinematic representation.

Al-Qadhi and Pawar's work on "videoing the chaos of evacuation" represents a more sustained attempt to address the adaptation process directly [7, p. 1]. Their study asserts that "the videoing adaptation of the literary oeuvre can carry the fundamental theme in more apparent method than the written feat" [7, p. 1], suggesting that cinema possesses certain advantages in conveying the sensory and emotional dimensions of historical trauma. However, this claim is made without the rigorous comparative analysis that would substantiate it, and the study does not sufficiently engage with the theoretical challenges posed by adaptation, including questions of fidelity, medium specificity, and the politics of representation. The assertion that cinema achieves greater "apparency" requires careful examination of specific scenes, narrative structures, and character portrayals across both works.

The broader field of Partition narrative studies has generated important comparative work across different literary texts. Badiger's comparative study of *Train to Pakistan* and Bapsi Sidhwa's *The Pakistani Bride* examines how both novels recount "the horror stories of partition on both the sides" [6, p. 1], identifying recurring themes of displacement, communal violence, and human tragedy. Similarly, Sharma positions Singh's novel within a tradition of historical fiction, noting that "Indian novelists like Khushwant Singh, Salman Rushdie, Manohar Malgonkar, Amitav Ghosh have described in detail about the horrors of partition" [11, p. 1]. These comparative literary studies demonstrate the value of examining multiple texts within the same historical context, yet they do not extend their comparative methodology across media forms. The possibility of comparing literary and cinematic Partition narratives as distinct modes of historical representation remains largely unexplored.

The literature on film adaptation theory, as surveyed in Section 2.5, provides the necessary conceptual tools for such a comparative analysis. However, these theoretical frameworks have seldom been applied to the specific case of *Train to Pakistan*. Studies that examine adaptations of Partition narratives in general are scarce, and those that focus on Singh's novel specifically are even rarer. This gap is particularly significant given the distinctive challenges posed by adapting a novel that deals with historical trauma. As Al-Qadhi and Pawar note, "some concepts seem so difficult to be shifted into films as they bear so tragic, melancholic, or adventurous circumstances" [7, p. 1]. The adaptation of *Train to Pakistan* thus raises questions not only about technical processes of translation but also about the ethical responsibilities of representing violence and suffering across different media.

A further limitation of existing scholarship is its tendency toward either formalist analysis or thematic summary without adequate attention to the ideological dimensions of adaptation. Studies that compare the novel and film often focus on plot fidelity—what is retained, what is omitted—without interrogating the political implications of these choices. For instance, the representation of communal harmony in Mano Majra, the portrayal of individual characters such as Juggut Singh and Hukum Chand, and the treatment of love and sacrifice are all significant elements that undergo transformation in the adaptation process. A comparative analysis that attends to these specific transformations, while situating them within broader questions of historical representation, memory, and ideology, would contribute substantially to both Partition studies and adaptation theory.

In conclusion

**2.7 Research Gap**

Despite the substantial body of scholarship examining Khushwant Singh’s *Train to Pakistan* and its 1998 film adaptation directed by Pamela Rooks, significant lacunae persist in the comparative analysis of these two works. Existing studies have addressed the novel and the film separately or have identified surface-level similarities and differences without interrogating the deeper structural, thematic, and ideological transformations that occur during the process of adaptation. The present section delineates these research gaps and justifies the necessity of a systematic comparative investigation of the novel and its cinematic counterpart.

A first and fundamental gap concerns the absence of a sustained comparative analysis that extends beyond descriptive cataloguing of plot correspondences. Dr Manjula Goel’s study, for instance, enumerates similarities such as the central theme, setting in Mano Majra, the preservation of key characters like Jugga and Nooran, and the retention of pivotal events including the arrival of the ghost train [1, p. 1]. While such enumerations are useful, they remain largely classificatory rather than analytical. The study does not interrogate *why* certain elements are retained, omitted, or transformed, nor does it engage with the ideological implications of these choices. As Al-Qadhi and Pawar observe, film adaptation necessarily involves a subjective interpretation by the director, who “may insist on some passages of the story than others” and thereby craft a film that differs in pace, perspective, and emphasis [7, p. 5]. A rigorous comparative study must therefore move beyond simple inventory to examine how cinematic choices reconfigure the novel’s thematic and ideological fabric.

Furthermore, the existing scholarship on *Train to Pakistan* has largely neglected the role of film adaptation theory as a framework for understanding the shifts between literary and cinematic modes. While Goel’s work acknowledges that “the film inevitably condenses, streamlines, and emphasizes certain aspects for cinematic impact” [2, p. 1], it does not draw upon established adaptation theories—such as those proposed by George Bluestone, Robert Stam, or Linda Hutcheon—to explain these transformations. A comparative study that employs such theoretical lenses could illuminate how Rooks’ adaptation negotiates between fidelity to the source text and the imperatives of a visual medium, particularly in representing the novel’s central themes of violence, communalism, and humanism.

Another critical gap pertains to the under-analysis of character development and moral complexity across the two media. Singh’s novel devotes considerable narrative attention to the internal conflicts of figures like Jugga and Iqbal, exploring their psychological and moral evolution against the backdrop of communal violence. As one study notes, the novel is “more expansive in its treatment of time and characters,” delving “deeply into the backstories and motivations of the characters” [9, p. 1]. The film, by contrast, condenses these nuances through its temporal constraints and visual emphasis. Yet, no existing study has systematically compared how the moral crises experienced by characters—particularly Jugga’s transformation from outlaw to sacrificial hero or Iqbal’s idealism versus pragmatic disillusionment—are represented or reshaped in the cinematic adaptation. This omission is significant, given that the novel’s humanist critique of communalism depends heavily on the interiority of its characters, a dimension that film cannot replicate in identical form.

Moreover, existing research has not sufficiently explored how the film adaptation navigates the representation of communalism and moral values in relation to Singh’s original text. Dr Mala Srivastava’s analysis of communalism and moral values in the novel examines how “ordinary people who once lived together peacefully become victims of hatred and political manipulation” while simultaneously highlighting “the enduring power of humanity, sacrifice, love, and compassion” [12, p. 1]. However, this study, like others, confines its analysis to the novel alone. The film’s visual and auditory techniques—such as the use of close-ups, sound design, and mise-en-scène—necessarily shape the audience’s emotional and ethical engagement with communal violence. A comparative analysis could reveal whether Rooks’ adaptation reinforces, subverts, or complicates Singh’s moral vision, particularly in scenes depicting the ghost train, the escalation of inter-religious hostility, and Jugga’s ultimate sacrifice.

Additionally, the literature reveals a methodological gap insofar as most studies treat the novel and film as separate objects of inquiry rather than as interconnected texts that participate in a dialogic relationship. Scholars have applied Social Identity Theory to examine identity and belonging in Singh’s novel [10, p. 1] and have analyzed the depiction of post-Partition violence [6, p. 1], but these investigations rarely extend to the film adaptation. Similarly, studies of the film alone, such as those focusing on its realistic portrayal of evacuation [4, p. 1; 8, p. 1], do not systematically compare these portrayals with the novel’s textual strategies. The result is a fragmented body of scholarship that cannot account for the intermedial dynamics at play.

In light of these gaps, the present study aims to contribute a rigorous comparative analysis of Khushwant Singh’s *Train to Pakistan* and Pamela Rooks’ film adaptation, employing adaptation theory, close reading, and thematic analysis to examine how each medium represents violence, communalism, and humanism. By moving beyond descriptive cataloguing to interrogate the ideological and aesthetic choices inherent in the adaptation process, this research will illuminate the ways in which the film reconfigures the novel’s moral and political commitments for a different audience and historical moment.
